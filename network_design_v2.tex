\title{Cost, Carbon, and the Speed of Replenishment\\ in Distribution Network Design}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent A firm that distributes through regional distribution centers decides how many to operate and how
fast to replenish them. Each decision trades one source of emissions against another: slower line haul burns
less fuel but requires more safety stock, and more distribution centers shorten the last mile but add buildings
and give up the pooling that keeps inventory low. We model the two decisions jointly under a carbon price or cap
and show that the design problem has a single best solution at every carbon price. Whether carbon pricing makes the network faster or slower, and denser or more consolidated, depends on how
carbon intensity per dollar compares across holding, line-haul freight, last-mile delivery and facilities,
weighted by the share of a distribution center's cost that is lost pooling. We calibrate this comparison to
public US emission factors and logistics costs and find that it depends on how speed is bought and how the last
mile is driven. Speed bought with fuel --- faster vessels, truck instead of intermodal rail, or air --- emits more
per dollar than holding the stock of any product we examine, perishables included, so carbon pricing slows it.
Speed bought with labor, such as team drivers, adds little carbon and is favored. Heavy diesel delivery favors more distribution centers, and electric
vans fewer.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Distribution network design; Carbon pricing; Replenishment speed; Safety stock; Last-mile delivery; Continuous approximation

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Distribution networks are being redesigned on two fronts. Firms are moving stock closer to demand. Amazon divided its US fulfillment network into eight regions and
reported a $19\%$ reduction in the miles traveled to deliver packages \citep{amazon2023q2}; Home Depot is
building flatbed distribution centers in its forty largest markets \citep{homedepot2020fdc}. Firms are also
slowing their line haul. AstraZeneca has moved most of its international freight from air to ocean and ships
about $65\%$ of its cargo by sea \citep{pharmcommerce2023astrazeneca}, and truckload shippers typically convert
lanes to intermodal rail once the savings reach $10$ to $15\%$ \citep{freightwaves2026intermodal}. The reasons these firms give are mainly cost and delivery service. Carbon now bears on such decisions as well:
the European Union has priced the carbon of maritime shipping since 2024 \citep{eu2023ets} and will price road
fuel from 2028 \citep{eea2026ets2}. California is phasing in the reporting of supply-chain emissions from 2027
\citep{california2023sb253}, and ISO 14083 standardizes how the emissions of transport and warehouses are
counted \citep{iso2023_14083}. A firm that prices or caps its
distribution carbon must therefore ask which way carbon pushes two basic design decisions: how many
distribution centers to operate, and how fast to replenish them.

A firm serves its markets from a network of distribution centers that it replenishes over time. Among the operational choices this involves, we study two: the \emph{density} of the network --- how many distribution centers the firm operates --- and the \emph{speed} with which those centers are replenished. Both shape the firm's cost, and both shape the carbon its distribution emits. With carbon priced and reported
\citep{mckinnon2012setting}, a firm sets both with carbon as well as cost in view, and we study how a carbon price
shapes that choice.

Each decision carries a standard cost--carbon trade-off. Network density governs proximity to markets: operating more distribution centers shortens the last-mile distance and changes the warehouse energy the network consumes. Replenishment speed governs availability: replenishing faster shortens the lead time that inventory must cover and changes the emissions of the freight that serves the network. Distribution emits carbon both when goods move and when they are held, and each affects both the firm's cost and its carbon. Cost balances these effects one way; with a price on carbon, the carbon-minimizing balance can fall elsewhere, and how it does so is the central question this paper addresses.

Slowing replenishment and consolidating the network are the common transport-side responses: slowing moves
freight by slower, lower-emission modes, as in slow steaming in shipping
\citep{psaraftis2013speed, cariou2011slow}, and consolidating serves markets from fewer, larger distribution
centers \citep{mckinnon2012setting}. Each response also affects inventory. A unit held in a warehouse consumes
energy --- refrigeration, climate control, handling --- for as long as it is held. Slowing replenishment
lengthens the lead time that a stock buffer must cover, so the safety stock, and the carbon of holding it,
rises with the square root of that lead time; consolidating pools demand across markets
\citep{eppen1979effects} and saves facility energy while lengthening the last mile. Each substitution thus lowers one
source of carbon and raises another, and whether it lowers the total depends on which source is larger.

The choice between the two sources is a choice between two operational substitutions. A firm
provides \emph{availability} either by holding safety stock or by replenishing faster --- a shorter lead
time leaves less for the buffer to cover, so speed and inventory are substitutes for the same service ---
and it provides market \emph{proximity} either by operating more local facilities or by delivering farther
from fewer, so facilities and last-mile travel are substitutes for the same access. The cost-minimizing configuration uses one mix of
these activities; when carbon is priced, the optimal mix can differ. When safety stock is more
carbon-intensive per dollar than the inbound transport that would replace it, the carbon-minimizing chain
provides availability through \emph{faster} replenishment. When last-mile travel is more carbon-intensive
per dollar than the facilities that would shorten it, the carbon-minimizing chain provides proximity through
facilities --- a \emph{more dispersed} network. The lowest-carbon distribution system thus depends on the
product and, as we show, even more on how the firm buys speed and how it delivers the last mile.

We formalize this in a single-product location--inventory model with stochastic demand. The firm chooses
network density and replenishment speed to minimize cost subject to a carbon cap and a service-level
requirement. Cost and carbon each comprise the same four components --- facilities, holding, inbound
transport, and last-mile transport --- and cost also includes the financing of stock in transit. The model follows the location--inventory tradition
\citep{daskin2002inventory, shen2003joint} in a continuous-approximation form
\citep{daganzo2005logistics}, with a distinguishing addition: replenishment speed is a decision. Because
speed sets the lead time that stochastic safety stock must cover, it directly determines inventory
carbon and couples the four components together.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it characterizes the joint design. We show that the priced problem has a single best design at every
carbon price, although it is not jointly convex. The carbon-minimizing direction of both decisions then follows
from four carbon-intensity-per-dollar ratios and one cost share that the cost-minimizing design reveals --- the
share of a distribution center's marginal cost that is lost pooling.
Because faster replenishment recovers part of the pooling that dispersion gives up, the carbon intensity of
line-haul freight enters the choice of how many distribution centers to operate, a link that the analysis of
either decision alone does not reveal. Second, it measures the ratios. From public US emission factors and
logistics costs, activities whose cost is mostly energy emit far more carbon per dollar than those whose cost
is mostly labor or capital. The classification therefore depends on how speed is bought and how the last mile is driven. Under carbon
pricing, speed bought with fuel is slowed for every product we examine, perishables included, and speed bought
with labor is favored; heavy diesel delivery calls for more distribution centers, and electric vans for fewer. Third, it shows how far the design moves. The response to a carbon price grows with the share of carbon in each activity's priced cost, and that share is
small at prices in current use. In a calibrated national network, a price of \$100 per tonne changes network
carbon by less than one percent for ambient goods, whereas a stringent target at the carbon minimum multiplies the number of distribution centers several times when
stores are served by diesel trucks. A firm can compute the diagnostic from its own rate data, and the same ratios give the carbon
price at which carbon becomes a material share of each activity's cost.

the location--inventory, carbon-network-design, and inventory--transport--emissions literatures.
Section~\ref{sec:model} develops the model, Section~\ref{sec:analysis} establishes the results,
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} applies the results to calibrated US data, Section~\ref{sec:managerial} draws out the
managerial implications, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} concludes.
\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:related}

This study connects four streams: location--inventory design with risk pooling, carbon-aware network design,
the joint control of inventory and transport mode under emissions regulation, and carbon as a priced
operational cost in service-constrained inventory systems. Each develops an element of the two substitutions
examined here --- availability (inventory versus replenishment speed) and proximity (facilities versus
last-mile travel). Each of these streams is well developed, and we draw on all four; we position the contribution by what it adds to each. The paper adds the joint design of network density and
replenishment speed under a carbon cap and a service requirement, an exact classification of the direction in
which carbon moves both decisions, and a calibration of the carbon intensities that decide it.

\subsection{Location--inventory design and risk pooling}

A first stream establishes how demand risk pooling shapes distribution network design: jointly choosing
facility location and safety stock, it characterizes the cost-optimal number and placement of distribution
centers. \citet{eppen1979effects} characterizes the effect of centralization on expected cost in the
multi-location newsvendor, establishing the square-root relationship between the number of stocking locations
and pooled safety stock. \citet{daskin2002inventory} and \citet{shen2003joint} embed working and safety stock
into the facility-location decision, so that risk pooling shapes the optimal number and placement of
distribution centers, with extensions to stochastic settings by \citet{snyder2007stochastic}, to capacitated
warehouses by \citet{ozsen2008capacitated}, and to continuous-approximation allocation by
\citet{tsao2012continuous}. \citet{daganzo2005logistics} provides the continuous-approximation foundation in
which last-mile distance scales with the inverse square root of the number of facilities. Closest to our spatial--inventory
coupling, \citet{straubert2024continuous} ties the number of stocking locations to an exact
reorder-point/order-quantity inventory model through a delivery-lead-time penalty, solved by continuous
approximation. Recent work extends the location--inventory problem in directions close to ours. \citet{hu2025location} add the choice of storage temperature, trading cooling cost against the quality decay of
fresh products in a network with risk pooling, and \citet{jalal2026integrated} integrate multi-period location,
inventory, and transportation decisions under demand uncertainty for a pharmaceutical network.
\citet{puttemans2025integration} review this literature and call for more realistic modeling assumptions; our
calibration in Section~\ref{sec:numerical} anchors each carbon rate in public data. The centralize-versus-decentralize question these models pose can also depend on forces beyond demand pooling: \citet{schmitt2015centralization} show analytically that supply-disruption risk diversification
can favor decentralization for a risk-averse firm, in contrast to the classical pooling result. These works
develop network density and safety-stock placement under risk pooling, with a cost or service objective and no
carbon; they do not make replenishment speed a continuous decision coupled with carbon. The present study
retains the risk-pooling structure and the density decision and adds an endogenous replenishment speed together
with a carbon objective.

\subsection{Carbon-aware network design}

A second stream brings carbon into facility location and network design, characterizing how an emissions
objective or cap can change the optimal network configuration. \citet{cachon2014retail}
relates retail store density to greenhouse gas cost, with consumer travel to stores as the dominant emission
channel and inventory entering through retail floor space. \citet{turken2017facility} examine facility location
and capacity under a carbon tax and emission limits, weighing centralization against decentralization through
production scale economies; \citet{harris2011assessing} compare cost-optimal and emissions-aware network
designs numerically; \citet{wang2020carbon} develop a carbon-compliant location--inventory model under
demand and carbon-price uncertainty; \citet{li2017joint} jointly optimize distribution-network design and
two-echelon inventory under a CO\textsubscript{2} emission tax; and \citet{hacardiaux2020assessing} assess the
transport-emission savings of a shared, consolidated location--inventory network under horizontal cooperation,
with \citet{hacardiaux2022balancing} balancing partner preferences over logistics cost and carbon footprint
in the same cooperative setting. Closest to ours in method, \citet{zhang2026economic} combine continuous
approximation with integer programming to show how a carbon price reshapes the choice among drone,
truck--drone, and truck last-mile delivery.
These studies make density or facility configuration the central decision and bring emissions into the
objective, the constraints, or an ex post assessment; where transport is modeled as an operational choice, it
enters through travel distances, discrete modes, or lead-time parameters rather than through a continuously
optimized replenishment speed chosen jointly with density.
The present study treats network density and replenishment speed as joint decisions and resolves the proximity
substitution between facilities and last-mile travel analytically.

\subsection{Inventory, transport mode, and emissions}

A third stream couples transport-mode and lead-time choices with inventory control under emissions regulation,
characterizing how carbon reshapes the trade-off between freight and the inventory it serves.
\citet{hoen2014effect} and \citet{chen2016effects} study transport mode selection alongside
inventory under stochastic demand and emissions regulation; \citet{qi2022transport} select door-to-door transport
services and routes for China--Europe freight against transport cost, in-transit inventory cost, and carbon; \citet{bektas2011pollution} formalize the
load--emission relationship in the pollution-routing problem, embedding fuel use and emissions as a function
of vehicle load in the routing objective; \citet{moradi2025load} optimize driving speed and path selection
under load- and grade-dependent fuel use at the routing level; \citet{konur2014integrated} solve the economic
order quantity model with less-than-truckload and truckload carriers under carbon cap, tax, and cap-and-trade
regulation, and \citet{schaefer2015economic} extend this to a continuous-review, reorder-point inventory system under stochastic
demand, characterizing the cost--emission Pareto frontier and analyzing lead-time and demand-variance
sensitivity. \citet{drent2023efficient} analyze dynamic dual supply-mode selection under an emission target,
and \citet{hammami2015carbon} bring carbon from manufacturing, ordering, and inventory into a multi-echelon
production--inventory model with lead-time constraints. In shipping, \citet{maloni2013slow} show by simulation
that slower vessel speed lowers both total cost and carbon on an Asia--North America lane while raising the pipeline inventory that shippers carry. Our speed threshold prices the carbon as well as the cost of
the inventory that slower replenishment requires. Across these papers, transport service is represented
through route/load physics, carrier class, discrete supply mode, or lead-time parameters and constraints; replenishment
speed is not a continuous decision chosen jointly with network density. The present study adds to this stream the continuous speed--density
threshold that results when replenishment speed and network density are chosen jointly under a carbon cap
and a service requirement.

\citet{boone2025freight} choose a discrete freight mode against inventory under an emission cap at a single
location, with no network-density decision. The present study replaces the finite mode set with a continuous
replenishment-speed decision, prices carbon on the holding as well as the transport side, embeds it in a network
whose density is chosen jointly, and characterizes the resulting continuous speed--density threshold and
complementarity.

\subsection{Carbon policy, service, and safety-stock placement}

A fourth stream prices carbon directly into inventory and service decisions, showing how a carbon price or cap
shifts order quantities and safety-stock placement.
\citet{hua2011managing} and \citet{bouchery2012including} develop carbon-aware order-quantity models under
cap-and-trade and multi-objective formulations, and \citet{benjaafar2013carbon} position cap, tax, and
cap-and-trade as priced operational instruments in simple supply-chain models; \citet{chen2013carbon} derive the
closed-form carbon-constrained economic order quantity, the single-location analog of the marginal
carbon-intensity logic we extend to a network, and \citet{arikan2014single} impose a product carbon-footprint
constraint on a dual-sourcing newsvendor. \citet{silbermayr2017inventory} bring environmental constraints into
inventory pooling with dependent demand. Within multi-echelon service models,
\citet{ni2015tradeoff} study the trade-off between service time and carbon in safety-stock placement under the
guaranteed-service framework, and \citet{wang2025inventory} incorporate cap-and-trade into guaranteed-service
placement. Probabilistic carbon limits have been modeled through chance constraints in lot sizing
\citep{koca2021stochastic} and in supply-chain network design \citep{shaw2016low}, typically inside large
mixed-integer programs; we instead obtain a closed-form deterministic equivalent that scales the transport component by a single factor. A recent review \citep{sebatjane2025sustainable} surveys carbon-aware inventory
models and the regulatory instruments they treat; across this body of work, transport-emission modeling, service and inventory, network design, and demand or
carbon uncertainty have developed in largely separate streams, and we bring these strands together. The service-placement models in particular price carbon against service
and safety stock, with the joint design of network density and replenishment speed outside their scope. 

Prior work covers carbon-aware density, mode and lead-time choice, service-constrained placement, and chance
constraints on carbon. The present study couples endogenous replenishment speed with network density under carbon and service
constraints and shows that the joint design is unique at every carbon price. It classifies the carbon-minimizing
direction of both decisions exactly. A speed threshold compares the carbon intensity per dollar of the inventory saving from speed --- holding,
weighted by the safety-stock share of that saving --- with that of the speed it buys; a density threshold compares last-mile intensity with a pooling-weighted blend of
facility, holding, and line-haul intensities; and complementarity links the two.
It then estimates these intensities from public data.
\section{The Model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Operational environment}

\paragraph{Region and markets.} A firm serves $N$ markets distributed over a service region of area $A$.
Per-period demand at market $i$ is a random variable with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$,
identically distributed across markets with common pairwise correlation $\rho \in [0,1)$. Demand is
stationary and the setting is single-product. Markets and distribution centers are treated symmetrically, the standard
simplification that admits closed-form continuous-approximation results \citep{daganzo2005logistics}.


\paragraph{Distribution centers and geography.} The firm opens $n$ identical distribution centers (DCs), $1 \le n \le N$, each
serving a sub-region of area $A/n$. By the continuous-approximation spatial law, the mean last-mile
(DC-to-market) distance falls as the square root of the served area,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dn}
  d(n) \;=\; \delta\, n^{-1/2}, \qquad \delta \propto \sqrt{A},
\end{equation}
so a denser network shortens delivery but only at a decreasing rate \citep{daganzo2005logistics}.

\paragraph{Replenishment and the speed decision.} Each DC is replenished directly from the source at a chosen
speed $v \in [v_{\min}, v_{\max}]$. Individual DCs lie at different distances from the source; what enters
cost and carbon is total inbound freight: with identical DCs each carrying throughput $N\mu/n$, this is
$\sum_j (N\mu/n)\,D_j = N\mu\,\bar D$, the total throughput times the \emph{mean} source-to-DC distance
$\bar D$. Because the DCs fill a region of fixed
size served from a fixed source, $\bar D$ is set by the region's geometry (order $\sqrt A$) and is
approximately invariant to $n$; we set $D \equiv \bar D$ and treat it as constant.\footnote{Two
modeling reductions are embedded here. First, the symmetric-DC reduction assigns every DC the representative lead
time $L = \bar D/v$ in place of its own $L_j = D_j/v$. Safety stock enters through $\sqrt{L}$, which is
concave in distance, so by Jensen's inequality $\sqrt{\bar D} \ge n^{-1}\sum_j \sqrt{D_j}$
\citep{jensen1906fonctions}: using the mean distance slightly \emph{overstates} aggregate safety stock, by a
gap that is second-order in the dispersion of the $D_j$ and reduces as the distances equalize. Second, replenishment is
point-to-point; were the DCs served on a consolidated route, the inbound distance would grow as $\sqrt{n}$ in
the manner of the store-delivery tour of \citet{cachon2014retail}, adding a term that reinforces centralization
in Proposition~\ref{prop:density} and leaving the fixed-density speed comparison of
Proposition~\ref{prop:speed} unchanged; with inbound distance varying in $n$, however, the speed response
would depend on density through $D(n)$, so the complementarity of Proposition~\ref{prop:complement} need not
carry over unchanged.}
The replenishment lead time is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lead}
  L(v) \;=\; D/v .
\end{equation}
Faster replenishment is simultaneously more expensive and more carbon-intensive. The maintained assumption
is only that the inbound cost rate $t(v)$ and emission rate $\varepsilon(v)$ (both per unit per mile) are
increasing and convex in $v$; for the closed-form results, we use the quadratic instantiation
$\varepsilon(v)=\varepsilon_0 v^2$ and $t(v)=t_0+t_1 v^2$, the canonical form motivated by the cubic
fuel-speed law of slow steaming \citep{psaraftis2013speed, cariou2011slow}. We interpret $v$ as a general index
of replenishment speed on a given origin--destination lane. Services on the lane --- intermodal rail, truckload
with a solo driver, or truckload with a team of two drivers who keep the truck moving about twice as many hours a
day --- differ in the lead time they give, and slow steaming is one motivating case. Treating replenishment
speed as a continuously optimized decision, chosen jointly with network density, couples the transport and
facility-and-holding components in this distribution setting.

\paragraph{Fuel and other costs of replenishment.} Services differ in how they shorten lead time. The
transport cost rate divides into fuel and other costs, $t(v)=t^{\mathrm f}(v)+t^{\mathrm o}(v)$. With a fixed
fuel price and emission factor, and with fuel accounting for the emissions that vary with speed,
$\varepsilon'(v)=\chi_{\mathrm f}\,t^{\mathrm f\prime}(v)$, where $\chi_{\mathrm f}$ is the carbon per dollar of fuel, so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rtfuel}
  \frac{\varepsilon'(v)}{t'(v)}=\chi_{\mathrm f}\,\xi(v),\qquad \xi(v)=\frac{t^{\mathrm f\prime}(v)}{t'(v)}.
\end{equation}
When both cost components rise with speed, $\xi(v)$ is the fuel share of the marginal cost of speed. Under the
quadratic instantiation, $t_1=b_{\mathrm f}+b_{\mathrm o}$ and $\varepsilon_0=\chi_{\mathrm f} b_{\mathrm f}$, where $b_{\mathrm f}v^2$ and $b_{\mathrm o}v^2$ are the
speed-dependent fuel and other costs, so that $\varepsilon_0/t_1$ is constant. Moving from intermodal rail to
truckload raises fuel use per unit-mile, whereas moving from a solo driver to a team raises driver cost at nearly
the same fuel use per mile. Where $t$ is a freight rate, the relation assumes that fuel costs pass through to the
rate.

\paragraph{Inventory and service.} Each DC operates a base-stock policy and must meet a cycle service level
$\alpha$ --- the in-stock probability at the end of a period, the Type-1 (cycle) service measure. With normally distributed demand the safety factor is $z = \Phi^{-1}(\alpha)$, with $\alpha>\tfrac12$ so
that $z>0$, taken here as a service requirement (a parameter). Demand is independent across periods, and each DC reviews its
inventory continuously, so safety stock protects against demand over the replenishment lead time; under periodic
review the protection interval would also include the review period. A DC that pools $g = N/n$ markets faces aggregate per-period demand with
standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{g\,[\,1+(g-1)\rho\,]}$, the statistical economy of scale in inventory
\citep{eppen1979effects}. Covering lead-time demand, each DC holds safety stock
$z\,\sigma\sqrt{L}\,\sqrt{g[1+(g-1)\rho]}$. Summing over the $n$ DCs and defining the \emph{pooling expression}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi}
  \Psi(n) \;=\; N\rho + n(1-\rho),
\end{equation}
the system-wide safety stock is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ss}
  \sstock(n,v) \;=\; z\,\sigma\,\sqrt{D/v}\;\sqrt{N\,\Psi(n)} .
\end{equation}
At $\rho = 0$, \eqref{eq:ss} reduces to $z\sigma\sqrt{L}\sqrt{nN}$, the square-root law: decentralizing
from $1$ to $N$ DCs multiplies safety stock by $\sqrt{N}$. As $\rho \to 1$, $\Psi(n) \to N$ and safety stock
becomes invariant to $n$ --- the pooling benefit vanishes.

\paragraph{Decisions.} The firm chooses the network density $n \in [1,N]$ (relaxed to a continuous
variable and rounded) and the replenishment speed $v \in [v_{\min}, v_{\max}]$. Service is a requirement,
so $\alpha$ (hence $z$) is a parameter in our model. Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects the notation.

\subsection{Cost and carbon}

Both the cost and carbon functions are system-wide flows over a single
representative period, consistent with the stationary single-product environment above:
$F,\varphi$ are charged per DC per period, $h,\eta$ per unit held per period, and the transport terms accrue
against the per-period throughput. The representative distances in Figure~\ref{fig:setting} therefore enter
global cost and carbon through aggregate freight tasks, not through a per-territory objective. Throughput
itself is conserved at $N\mu$ regardless of the configuration: the firm moves the mean demand of all $N$
markets through the network, whatever density $n$ and speed $v$ it chooses. With per-period throughput
$N\mu$,
\begin{align}
  K(n,v) &= \underbrace{F\,n}_{\text{fixed}} + \underbrace{h\,\sstock(n,v)}_{\text{holding}} + \underbrace{h_p\,P(v)}_{\text{in transit}}
            + \underbrace{t(v)\,N\mu D}_{\text{inbound}} + \underbrace{t_L\,N\mu\,d(n)}_{\text{last-mile}},
            \label{eq:K}\\[2pt]
  C(n,v) &= \underbrace{\varphi\,n}_{\text{fixed}} + \underbrace{\eta\,\sstock(n,v)}_{\text{holding}}
            + \underbrace{\varepsilon(v)\,N\mu D}_{\text{inbound}} + \underbrace{\varepsilon_L\,N\mu\,d(n)}_{\text{last-mile}},
            \label{eq:C}
\end{align}
where $F,\varphi$ are fixed cost/carbon per DC, $h,\eta$ are holding cost/carbon per unit per period,
$h_p$ is the financing cost of a unit in transit per period, $P(v)=N\mu D/v$ is the stock in transit, and
$t_L,\varepsilon_L$ are last-mile cost/carbon rates \citep{shen2003joint, hua2011managing,
benjaafar2013carbon}. Each term multiplies a rate by the activity it prices; apart from the financing of stock in transit, which
carries no carbon, the two functions differ only in whether the rate is measured in dollars or kilograms.

\paragraph{Facilities.} The facility terms $F n$ and $\varphi n$ are the periodic cost and carbon of operating
the open DCs. They rise linearly in $n$ and do not depend on $v$. Facility carbon $\varphi$ is distinct from
holding carbon $\eta$: $\varphi$ captures baseline building energy and embodied carbon, whereas $\eta$ prices
the incremental carbon of storing a unit (for example refrigeration or handling).

\paragraph{Holding.} The holding terms $h\sstock(n,v)$ and $\eta\sstock(n,v)$ value the system-wide safety
stock of \eqref{eq:ss}. They rise in $n$ through the loss of pooling in $\Psi(n)$ and fall in $v$ through the
lead-time factor $\sqrt{D/v}$. This is the channel through which replenishment speed couples transport and
inventory carbon; for cost, stock in transit is a second channel.

\paragraph{Stock in transit.} Goods in transit, $P(v)=N\mu D/v$, fall as replenishment speeds up. A firm that
owns them while they travel carries their financing cost, $h_p$ per unit per period --- the capital charge on the
value of the goods --- and $h_p=0$ when title passes on receipt at the distribution center. Stock in transit
occupies no warehouse, and the carbon of moving it is counted in the inbound transport term, so it adds no
carbon of its own. Its financing cost does not depend on $n$. Because that cost carries no carbon, it lowers the
carbon intensity of the inventory saving that speed obtains: at a configuration $(n,v)$, the share of that
saving that is safety stock is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stockshare}
  a(n,v)=\frac{h\,\sstock(n,v)}{h\,\sstock(n,v)+2h_p\,P(v)}\in(0,1],
\end{equation}
where the factor two reflects that safety stock falls with the square root of the lead time and stock in transit
with the lead time itself. Without financing, $a=1$; the share rises with speed.

\paragraph{Inbound transport.} The inbound terms multiply the representative freight task $N\mu D$ by the
speed-dependent rates $t(v)$ and $\varepsilon(v)$. Since $D$ is treated as invariant to $n$, inbound transport
does not vary with network density; it rises in $v$ through the increasing convex rates
$t(v)$ and $\varepsilon(v)$, opposing the holding term that falls in $v$.

\paragraph{Last-mile transport.} The last-mile terms multiply throughput $N\mu$ by the mean DC-to-market
distance $d(n)=\delta n^{-1/2}$ and the rates $t_L,\varepsilon_L$. A denser network shortens this leg at the
continuous-approximation rate, so the last-mile term is decreasing and convex in $n$ and independent of $v$
under constant per-mile last-mile rates.

\paragraph{Decomposition and scope.} The carbon function splits into a transport component
$\varepsilon(v)N\mu D+\varepsilon_LN\mu d(n)$ and a facility-and-holding component $\varphi n+\eta\sstock(n,v)$;
the cost function has the analogous partition, with the financing term $h_pP(v)$ in addition. Slowing replenishment reduces inbound transport carbon but
raises safety stock, while dispersion reduces last-mile distance but raises facility count and pooling loss.
Two scope points follow. Cycle stock depends on the replenishment quantity, which we hold fixed, and is
invariant to both decisions. Production and procurement are excluded because throughput is fixed at $N\mu$ and
therefore constant in both decisions.

\subsection{The program}

The firm minimizes cost subject to a carbon cap $B$ and the service requirement,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pcap}
  \min_{\,1\le n\le N,\ v_{\min}\le v\le v_{\max}}\ K(n,v)
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad C(n,v)\le B .
\end{equation}
Here $K$ and $C$ are expected flows over the model's accounting period: under the stationary demand assumption they are the long-run average cost and carbon of a configuration, and they can be
scaled to an annual expected-carbon cap by multiplying by the number of periods. Because demand is random,
realized carbon over a specified accounting horizon fluctuates about its expectation; we develop the analysis
for the expected-value cap and return below to a chance-constrained cap that bounds realized carbon with
specified confidence (Proposition~\ref{prop:chance}).

Equivalently, pricing carbon at $\tau \ge 0$ gives the priced objective $J=K+\tau C$. At an interior optimum,
the cap multiplier equals the price ($\lambda=\tau$), as in carbon-constrained inventory models
\citep{chen2013carbon}; sweeping $\tau$ traces the cost--carbon frontier, along which the optimal configuration moves continuously
(Proposition~\ref{prop:unique}). Because cost and carbon are convex in the logarithms of $n$ and $v$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:unique}), the capped problem \eqref{eq:Pcap} is a convex program in those variables: every
cap above the carbon minimum is met by a finite carbon price, and the capped problem and the problem under that
price share the same unique optimum. The carbon minimum itself is the limit as the price grows without bound. Jumps arise only from the
integer number of distribution centers. Define the blended rates
\[
  F_\tau=F+\tau\varphi,\qquad H_\tau=h+\tau\eta,\qquad
  T_\tau(v)=t(v)+\tau\varepsilon(v),\qquad
  \ell_\tau=t_L+\tau\varepsilon_L .
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:J}
  J(n,v;\tau) \;=\; F_\tau n + H_\tau\,\sstock(n,v) + h_p\,P(v)
     + T_\tau(v)N\mu D + \ell_\tau N\mu d(n).
\end{equation}
The financing cost of stock in transit carries no carbon and is not blended. Setting $\tau=0$ recovers cost;
the limit $\tau\to\infty$ recovers carbon up to scale.
\section{Analysis}\label{sec:analysis}

We now characterize how carbon pricing changes the joint design. We first show that the design problem has
a unique optimum at every carbon price (Proposition~\ref{prop:unique}). The first-order conditions then
decompose into two marginal substitutions, each analyzed with the other decision held fixed:
Proposition~\ref{prop:speed} gives the speed threshold on the availability margin,
Proposition~\ref{prop:service} records the service and volatility comparative static, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:density} gives the pooling-adjusted density threshold on the proximity margin.
Proposition~\ref{prop:complement} establishes the complementarity through which the two margins interact, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:classify} combines them into an exact classification of the carbon-minimizing direction
of both decisions. Corollary~\ref{cor:nopool} gives the magnitudes in terms of the four ratios and the share $a_K$ when pooling
is absent, in closed form without financing, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:path} describes how the design moves as the carbon price rises.
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} applies them to a calibrated network. Proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\begin{lemma}[Carbon intensity per dollar]\label{lem:blend}
Pricing carbon at $\tau$ replaces each cost rate in \eqref{eq:K} by the blended rate
$\mathrm{rate}_\$ + \tau\,\mathrm{rate}_{\mathrm{kg}}$, yielding the objective $J$ of \eqref{eq:J}; the financing
cost of stock in transit is not blended. In the interior comparisons below, each holding the other decision
fixed, the \emph{direction} in which the carbon-minimizing choice departs from the cost-minimizing one is
governed by four carbon-intensity-per-dollar ratios --- holding $\eta/h$, inbound transport at the margin
$\varepsilon'/t'$ of \eqref{eq:rtfuel} ($\varepsilon_0/t_1$ under the quadratic instantiation), last-mile
$\varepsilon_L/t_L$, and facilities $\varphi/F$ --- and, on the speed margin, by
the safety-stock share $a$ of \eqref{eq:stockshare}, while the magnitude and the realized joint optimum also
depend on pooling, the bounds, and the complementarity of the two decisions.
\end{lemma}

\noindent The carbon-minimizing choice on each decision therefore depends on each activity's carbon intensity
\emph{relative to its cost}, not on its absolute rates.

With $G(n,v)=\partial\sstock(n,v)/\partial n$ denoting the marginal safety stock of an additional DC,
differentiation gives the stationarity conditions
\begin{align}
  F_\tau + H_\tau G(n,v) &= \frac{\ell_\tau N\mu\delta}{2}\,n^{-3/2}, \label{eq:focn}\\[2pt]
  N\mu D\,T_\tau'(v) &= \frac{H_\tau\,\sstock(n,v)}{2v}+\frac{h_p\,P(v)}{v}, \label{eq:focv}
\end{align}
the first balancing the marginal facility and lost-pooling costs against the marginal last-mile saving, and
the second balancing the marginal transport cost of speed against the marginal holding and financing saving it
obtains.
The joint first-order conditions thus reveal two marginal substitutions. The speed condition
\eqref{eq:focv} governs the \emph{availability margin}: safety stock and replenishment speed are
alternative ways to provide service. The density condition \eqref{eq:focn} governs the \emph{proximity
margin}: facilities and last-mile distance are alternative ways to reach markets. The thresholds below
compare the carbon intensity per dollar of the activities on each margin, and complementarity determines how
the two margins interact in the joint configuration.

The stationarity conditions identify the optimum because the design problem has a unique optimum at every
carbon price. The objective is not jointly convex in $n$ and $v$, but it is convex in their logarithms. Let
$J^{\mathrm r}(n;\tau)=\min_{v_{\min}\le v\le v_{\max}}J(n,v;\tau)$ be the \emph{reduced objective}, the
cost, carbon charge included, of operating $n$ distribution centers when speed is chosen optimally for that
network. A function
on an interval is \emph{strictly quasiconvex} if it strictly decreases and then strictly increases, either
phase possibly empty, so that it has a single minimizer.

\begin{proposition}[A unique optimum]\label{prop:unique}
Let $t$ and $\varepsilon$ be increasing, differentiable, and convex, let all cost and carbon rates be
positive, let $h_p\ge0$, and let $v_{\min}>0$. For every carbon price $\tau\ge0$, and for the carbon objective $C$:
\textup{(i)} the objective is strictly convex in $(\ln n,\ln v)$ on the feasible region;
\textup{(ii)} the problem has a unique minimizer, and under a carbon price the minimizer
$\big(n^*(\tau),v^*(\tau)\big)$ is continuous in $\tau$;
\textup{(iii)} the speed response $v^*(n)$ is unique, continuous, and nondecreasing in $n$, and the objective at
any fixed speed and the reduced objective are strictly quasiconvex in $n$ on $[1,N]$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent Proposition~\ref{prop:unique} has a direct interpretation. Adding a distribution center shortens every delivery but adds a facility charge and the safety stock lost to
pooling. Measured in proportional changes of the network and of speed, each of these effects is convex, so once an
additional center stops paying for itself, no larger network makes it pay again. Three consequences follow. First, the stationarity conditions \eqref{eq:focn}--\eqref{eq:focv} identify the
optimum whenever it is interior. Second, the comparative statics below are global, not local to a particular
optimum. Third, in the continuous model the design moves without jumps as the carbon price rises, so
discontinuities arise only from the integer number of distribution centers. Because the reduced objective is flat near its minimum, treating that number as continuous costs little. In the calibrated network of Section~\ref{sec:numerical},
rounding it to the best integer raises the objective by at most $0.09\%$, in line with the accuracy reported for
continuous-approximation designs \citep{ouyang2006discretization, ansari2018advancements}.

\paragraph{Availability: inventory or speed.} The speed condition isolates the availability margin.
Increasing speed raises inbound transport cost and carbon but lowers the safety stock required for service;
the speed threshold identifies which activity is more carbon-intensive per dollar on this margin.

\begin{proposition}[Speed reversal]\label{prop:speed}
Fix the density $n$, and let the speed-dependent cost and carbon be
\[
  \hat K(v)=h\,\kappa(n)\,v^{-1/2}+h_pN\mu D\,v^{-1}+N\mu D\,t(v), \qquad \hat C(v)=\eta\,\kappa(n)\,v^{-1/2}+N\mu D\,\varepsilon(v),
\]
with $\kappa(n)=z\sigma\sqrt{ND}\,\sqrt{\Psi(n)}$. If $t$ and $\varepsilon$ are increasing,
differentiable, and convex, then at a common density and for
interior optima the carbon-minimizing speed exceeds the cost-minimizing speed if and only if the inventory saving
from speed is more carbon-intensive per dollar than inbound transport \emph{at the margin},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:speedthreshold}
  a(n,v_{\mathrm K})\,\frac{\eta}{h} \;>\; \frac{\varepsilon'(v_{\mathrm K})}{t'(v_{\mathrm K})},
\end{equation}
evaluated at the cost-minimizing speed $v_{\mathrm K}$. With the quadratic instantiation
$\varepsilon(v)=\varepsilon_0 v^2$, $t(v)=t_0+t_1 v^2$, the right side is $\varepsilon_0/t_1$. Without financing
($h_p=0$), $a=1$, the threshold reduces to $\eta/h>\varepsilon_0/t_1$, and the unconstrained optimum is closed
form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vstar}
  v^*(n) \;=\; \left[\frac{(h+\tau\eta)\,z\sigma\sqrt{ND}\,\sqrt{\Psi(n)}}
                       {4\,(t_1+\tau\varepsilon_0)\,N\mu D}\right]^{2/5}.
\end{equation}
On $[v_{\min},v_{\max}]$ the optima are the projections of the unconstrained speeds onto the interval; under
the threshold \eqref{eq:speedthreshold} the comparison $v_C\ge v_K$ then holds weakly, and strictly when
neither projection binds.
\end{proposition}

\noindent Financing stock in transit makes speed more valuable to the cost-minimizing firm but not to the
carbon-minimizing one, whose objective it does not enter; it lowers the share $a$ and so works against faster
replenishment under carbon. A lower fuel share of the marginal cost of speed, by \eqref{eq:rtfuel}, works the
other way. Under the quadratic instantiation, as the carbon price rises, the fixed-density speed optimum moves
monotonically between the cost and carbon endpoints, with or without financing (Proposition~\ref{prop:path}), so the endpoint comparison of the
proposition also orders every finite price.

\noindent Total carbon is U-shaped in speed. When the inventory saving from speed is more carbon-intensive per
dollar than the freight that would replace it, a tighter carbon target can select \emph{faster} replenishment.
Volatility and the service target raise the level of speed at both optima. Under the quadratic
instantiation, without financing they leave its direction unchanged, and with financing they raise the share $a$
and so can turn the comparison toward faster replenishment (Proposition~\ref{prop:service}). The comparison is at common density; in the joint problem, the density channel reinforces the
speed reversal when carbon also disperses the network and attenuates it otherwise, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:classify} separates the two effects exactly.

Service and volatility enter through the same channel. We use $J^{\mathrm r}(n;s)\equiv J\big(n,v^*(n;s);s\big)$,
with $s=z\sigma$, to denote the reduced objective obtained after
substituting the speed response into the priced objective.

\begin{proposition}[Service and volatility as carbon parameters]\label{prop:service}
Under the quadratic instantiation, $v^*(n)$ is increasing in the safety factor $z=\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)$ and in
the demand standard deviation $\sigma$ wherever it is interior, and at every $n$ both raise the holding term in
\eqref{eq:focn}. Consequently, at every carbon price, a higher
$z$ or $\sigma$ raises the inventory penalty of a denser network and lowers the optimal density, strictly when
it is interior; in particular, it shifts the carbon-minimizing density toward consolidation. With financing
($h_p>0$), a higher $z$ or $\sigma$ also raises the share $a(n,v_{\mathrm K})$, so at a common density it can turn
the comparison \eqref{eq:speedthreshold} toward faster replenishment but never toward slower.
\end{proposition}

\noindent A service requirement therefore affects the carbon-minimizing design as well as inventory cost;
endogenous service choice is a natural extension (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Proximity: facilities or last-mile travel.} The density condition isolates the proximity
margin. Increasing density shortens the last mile but raises facility cost and carbon and weakens pooling;
the density threshold identifies when carbon places more weight on last-mile distance than on the
facility-and-pooling cost of proximity, and pooling keeps the comparison from reducing to a single pair of
rates.

\begin{proposition}[Density and dispersion]\label{prop:density}
Fix the speed $v$ and let
\begin{equation}
  G(n,v)\;=\;\frac{\partial\,\sstock(n,v)}{\partial n}
           \;=\;z\sigma\sqrt{D/v}\,\sqrt{N}\,\frac{1-\rho}{2\sqrt{\Psi(n)}}\;\ge\;0
\end{equation}
be the marginal safety-stock penalty of an additional DC. The minimizer of $J(\cdot,v)$ is unique; it is
interior if and only if $J_n(1,v)<0<J_n(N,v)$, and otherwise lies at a boundary, $n^*\in\{1,N\}$. For an
interior cost-minimizing density $n_K(v)$, the carbon-minimizing density exceeds it, $n_C(v)>n_K(v)$, if and
only if last-mile transport is more carbon-intensive per dollar than the \emph{pooling-adjusted facility threshold},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:densthreshpool}
  \frac{\varepsilon_L}{t_L} \;>\; \frac{\varphi+\eta\,G\big(n_K(v),v\big)}{F+h\,G\big(n_K(v),v\big)} .
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is a weighted average of the facility ratio $\varphi/F$ and the holding ratio $\eta/h$,
with weight on $\eta/h$ rising in the pooling penalty $G$: it equals $\varphi/F$ when pooling is absent and
tilts monotonically toward $\eta/h$ as $G$ grows (lower correlation, higher safety factor or volatility, or
slower replenishment). In the no-pooling limit ($\rho\to1$, so $G\to0$) the optimum is closed form when interior,
$n^*=(\ell_\tau N\mu\delta/2F_\tau)^{2/3}$ (with the blended last-mile and facility rates
$\ell_\tau=t_L+\tau\varepsilon_L$ and $F_\tau=F+\tau\varphi$ of \eqref{eq:J}), and
\eqref{eq:densthreshpool} reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:densitythreshold}
  \frac{\varepsilon_L}{t_L} \;>\; \frac{\varphi}{F}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\noindent The density decision is the centralize-versus-decentralize question in carbon terms. By
\eqref{eq:densthreshpool}, the carbon-minimizing network is more dispersed when the last-mile ratio
$\varepsilon_L/t_L$ exceeds a facility threshold that pooling shifts from $\varphi/F$ toward $\eta/h$.
Pooling therefore enters as an explicit weight on the holding ratio, not as a uniform centralizing force: when
holding is more carbon-intensive per dollar than facilities, stronger pooling raises the threshold for
dispersion; otherwise it lowers it. A corner optimum, when it arises, is fully centralized or fully
decentralized.

\paragraph{Complementarity of speed and density.} The two margins interact through safety stock: faster
replenishment reduces the inventory penalty of density, and higher density increases the inventory exposure
affected by speed. Proposition~\ref{prop:complement} makes the interaction precise.

\begin{proposition}[Complementarity]\label{prop:complement}
For $\rho<1$, the speed response $v^*(n)$ is nondecreasing in $n$ and the density response $n^*(v)$ is
nondecreasing in $v$, each strictly where it is interior.
\end{proposition}

\noindent Faster replenishment shrinks safety stock and lowers the inventory penalty of dispersion; a denser
network raises the inventory at stake and raises the value of speed. Speed investments and network density are
therefore best evaluated jointly.

\paragraph{The joint design.} The marginal thresholds hold one decision fixed. In the joint design both
decisions adjust, and complementarity links them: a carbon price that disperses the network also raises the
value of speed, and one that speeds replenishment lowers the inventory penalty of dispersion.
Proposition~\ref{prop:classify} resolves the joint comparison exactly. We use $r_h=\eta/h$, $r_t=\varepsilon_0/t_1$, $r_L=\varepsilon_L/t_L$, and $r_F=\varphi/F$ to denote the four
ratios of Lemma~\ref{lem:blend}, so that the two diagnostic ratios are
\[
  R_{\mathrm{speed}}=\frac{r_h}{r_t},\qquad R_{\mathrm{density}}=\frac{r_L}{r_F},
\]
and let $(n_K,v_K)$ and $(n_C,v_C)$ denote the cost- and carbon-minimizing configurations, $\hat v_C$ the
carbon-minimizing speed at the cost-minimizing density $n_K$, and $a_K=a(n_K,v_K)$ the safety-stock share at the
cost minimum. Two quantities of the cost-minimizing network enter. The first is the \emph{pooling weight}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poolweight}
  \omega=\frac{h\,G(n_K,v_K)}{F+h\,G(n_K,v_K)}=1-\Big(\frac{n_K}{n_K^0}\Big)^{3/2},
  \qquad n_K^0=\Big(\frac{t_LN\mu\delta}{2F}\Big)^{2/3},
\end{equation}
the share of the marginal cost of a distribution center that is lost pooling rather than facility cost. Since
$n_K^0$ is the unconstrained density the firm would choose if pooling were absent, $\omega$ measures how far
pooling holds the network below that size. The second, for interior $n_K$, is the pair of \emph{speed bands}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:speedbands}
  \underline R=\sqrt{\Psi(n_K)/N}\;<\;1\;<\;\overline R=\sqrt{\Psi(n_K)/\Psi(1)},
\end{equation}
which bound how far a change in density can move the carbon-minimizing speed.

\begin{proposition}[Joint carbon-minimizing design]\label{prop:classify}
Suppose that $\rho<1$ and that the cost-minimizing density $n_K$ is interior.

\smallskip\noindent\textup{(i)} \emph{Density.} The carbon-minimizing network is more dispersed than the
cost-minimizing one, $n_C>n_K$, if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointdensgen}
  r_L\;>\;(1-\omega)\,r_F\;+\;\omega\,r_h\sqrt{v_K/\hat v_C},
\end{equation}
with $n_C=n_K$ at equality and $n_C<n_K$ when the inequality is reversed. Under the quadratic instantiation, and
when $v_K$ and $\hat v_C$ are interior, \eqref{eq:jointdensgen} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jointdens}
  r_L\;>\;(1-\omega)\,r_F\;+\;\omega\,r_h^{4/5}\,r_t^{1/5}\,a_K^{-1/5}.
\end{equation}

\smallskip\noindent\textup{(ii)} \emph{Speed.} Under the quadratic instantiation, and when the speed responses of
the cost and the carbon objective are interior at $n_K$ and at $n_C$, the carbon-minimizing speed satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:speeddecomp}
  \ln\frac{v_C}{v_K}=\frac25\ln\big(a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}\big)+\frac15\ln\frac{\Psi(n_C)}{\Psi(n_K)},
\end{equation}
so that $v_C>v_K$ if and only if $a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}>\sqrt{\Psi(n_K)/\Psi(n_C)}$.

\smallskip\noindent\textup{(iii)} \emph{Classification.} Under the conditions of (ii), if \eqref{eq:jointdens}
holds and $a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}\ge1$, the carbon-minimizing design is faster and more dispersed; if
\eqref{eq:jointdens} is reversed and $a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}\le1$, it is slower and more consolidated. Whatever
the density, it is faster if $a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}>\overline R$ and slower if $a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}<\underline R$.
Only when the two conditions point in opposite directions and $\underline R\le a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}\le\overline R$
does the direction of speed depend on the size of the density change; \eqref{eq:speeddecomp} then determines it.
At equality in \eqref{eq:jointdens}, the direction of speed is that of $a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}$ relative to one.
\end{proposition}

\noindent Proposition~\ref{prop:classify} answers, for a given product, the two questions a firm asks of its
network. The density condition \eqref{eq:jointdensgen} is Proposition~\ref{prop:density} with one change.
Adding a distribution center incurs a facility charge and lost pooling, in the proportions $1-\omega$ and
$\omega$ of its marginal cost. The carbon-minimizing firm holds the lost pooling at its own speed $\hat v_C$, and
safety stock falls with the square root of speed, so per dollar of lost pooling its carbon is
$r_h\sqrt{v_K/\hat v_C}$. The carbon-minimizing network is more dispersed when a dollar of last-mile delivery
emits more than this blend of facility and lost-pooling carbon, whether or not speed is at a bound. When the
speeds are interior, the carbon-minimizing speed at the cost-minimizing density, $\hat v_C$, differs from $v_K$
by the factor $(a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}})^{2/5}$, and the lost-pooling carbon becomes the weighted geometric mean in
\eqref{eq:jointdens}: along the carbon-minimizing speed response, an additional center raises holding carbon by
four-fifths of the carbon of lost pooling and inbound freight carbon by one-fifth. One consequence has no counterpart in the marginal
analysis: the carbon intensity of inbound freight bears on how many distribution centers to operate, through the
speed at which the carbon-minimizing firm holds the lost pooling. The effect is modest. It enters with the
pooling weight and the exponent one-fifth, and it vanishes where the carbon-minimizing speed stays at a bound.
Financing stock in transit raises the cost-minimizing speed, and with it the carbon of lost pooling, so it raises
the threshold for dispersion. The exponents are these shares of lost-pooling carbon, and without financing also the cost shares: with
freight cost and carbon proportional to $v^{p}$ rather than $v^2$, holding's share, and with it the exponent on
$r_h$, becomes $2p/(2p+1)$, and inbound freight's becomes $1/(2p+1)$, as does the magnitude of the exponent on
$a_K$.

\noindent The speed decomposition \eqref{eq:speeddecomp} separates the two reasons a carbon-minimizing chain
replenishes at a different speed. The intensity effect, $\tfrac25\ln(a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}})$, is the speed reversal
of Proposition~\ref{prop:speed}. The density effect, $\tfrac15\ln[\Psi(n_C)/\Psi(n_K)]$, is complementarity: a
more dispersed network holds more safety stock and so values speed more. The bands \eqref{eq:speedbands} bound
the density effect, because no network pools more than full consolidation or less than full dispersion. Whether
speed then changes also depends on whether either objective finds speed worth buying within the bounds.

\begin{corollary}[Magnitudes without pooling]\label{cor:nopool}
Under the quadratic instantiation, in the no-pooling limit ($\rho\to1$), $\omega=0$, the two decisions
separate, and for interior optima
\[
  \frac{n_C}{n_K}=R_{\mathrm{density}}^{2/3},\qquad \frac{v_C}{v_K}=\big(a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}\big)^{2/5}.
\]
Carbon separates into a proximity component $C_{\mathrm{prox}}(n)=\varphi n+\varepsilon_LN\mu\delta n^{-1/2}$ and
an availability component $C_{\mathrm{avail}}(v)=\eta z\sigma N\sqrt{D/v}+\varepsilon_0N\mu D\,v^2$, and the carbon of the
cost-minimizing design relative to the carbon minimum $C_{\min}$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:excess}
  \frac{C(n_K,v_K)}{C_{\min}}=\theta\,\frac{x+2x^{-1/2}}{3}+(1-\theta)\,\frac{4y^{-1/2}+y^2}{5},
  \qquad x=R_{\mathrm{density}}^{-2/3},\quad y=\big(a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}\big)^{-2/5},
\end{equation}
where $\theta=C_{\mathrm{prox}}(n_C)/C_{\min}$ is the proximity share of the minimum. To second order in the logarithms of the
ratios, the excess carbon of the cost-minimizing design is
$\theta\,(\ln R_{\mathrm{density}})^2/9+(1-\theta)\,\tfrac{2}{25}\big[\ln(a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}})\big]^2$.
\end{corollary}

\noindent Corollary~\ref{cor:nopool} gives the size of the redesign. Absent pooling and with both optima
interior, a product whose last mile is four times as carbon-intensive per dollar as its facilities runs
$4^{2/3}\approx2.5$ times as many distribution centers when minimizing carbon. To second order, the excess
carbon of the cost-minimizing design grows with the square of the logarithm of each ratio: it is small near parity and rises quickly away from it. The cost-minimizing network emits about $5\%$ more proximity carbon than the minimum when $R_{\mathrm{density}}=2$ and about $19\%$ more
when $R_{\mathrm{density}}=4$. Carbon-aware redesign therefore matters most for products whose ratios lie far
from one. With pooling the two decisions interact and these expressions are no longer exact;
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} illustrates the magnitudes in that case.

\paragraph{The carbon-price path.} A firm facing a rising carbon price needs to know how the design moves along
the way, not only where it ends. For an activity with carbon intensity per dollar $r_x$, we use $s_x(\tau)=\tau r_x/(1+\tau r_x)$ to describe the \emph{carbon share} of its blended rate --- for holding,
$s_h=\tau\eta/(h+\tau\eta)$. The share rises from zero to one as the price rises.

\begin{proposition}[Carbon-price path]\label{prop:path}
Under the quadratic instantiation:
\textup{(i)} at a fixed density and an interior speed response,
$\mathrm d\ln v^*/\mathrm d\ln\tau=(a_\tau s_h-s_t)/(3-a_\tau/2)$, where
$a_\tau=H_\tau\sstock/(H_\tau\sstock+2h_pP)$ at the optimum; its sign is that of $a(n,v^*)\,r_h-r_t$, so the
speed moves monotonically in $\tau$, and without financing the elasticity is $\tfrac25(s_h-s_t)$;
\textup{(ii)} in the no-pooling limit and for an interior density,
$\mathrm d\ln n^*/\mathrm d\ln\tau=\tfrac23\,(s_L-s_F)$;
\textup{(iii)} without financing, with pooling and interior speeds, if $r_L$ exceeds each of $r_F$, $r_h$, and
$r_t$, the optimal
density $n^*(\tau)$ is nondecreasing along the whole price path, and if $r_L$ falls short of each, it is
nonincreasing.
\end{proposition}

\noindent The carbon share of a blended rate measures how much of that activity's cost, carbon charge
included, is carbon. By part (i), at a given network and an interior speed, a rising carbon price speeds
replenishment exactly when the inventory saving from speed is more carbon-intensive per dollar than inbound
freight; without financing, exactly when carbon is a larger share of the cost of holding than of inbound freight.
The response is gradual: at a low price both shares are near zero and speed barely moves, while at a high price
both approach one and speed settles at its carbon-minimizing level. Part (iii) gives a condition a firm can
check once. Without financing, when its last mile is the most carbon-intensive of the four activities per
dollar and speeds are
interior, no increase in the carbon price calls for consolidation; when the last mile is the least
carbon-intensive, no increase calls for dispersion. Outside these two cases, part (iii) does not fix the direction along the
whole path.

\paragraph{Demand uncertainty: a chance-constrained cap.} The cost and carbon functions are expected
per-period flows, so the cap in \eqref{eq:Pcap} bounds \emph{expected} carbon. Because demand is random,
realized carbon fluctuates about $C(n,v)$: the facility charge and the base-stock safety-stock target are
planned and treated as deterministic, but inbound and last-mile freight scale with realized throughput
$Q=\sum_i X_i$, the sum of the realized market demands, which has mean $N\mu$ and variance
$\sigma_Q^2=\sigma^2 N\,[\,1+(N-1)\rho\,]$. Realized carbon is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ctilde}
  \tilde C(n,v) \;=\; \varphi n + \eta\,\sstock(n,v) + \big[\varepsilon(v)D+\varepsilon_L d(n)\big]\,Q,
\end{equation}
and a firm that requires realized carbon to stay within $B$ with confidence $1-\beta_c$ --- where $\beta_c$ is
the carbon-side tail probability, denoted with the subscript $c$ to distinguish it from the inventory
fill-rate $\beta$ of the lot-sizing literature --- imposes the chance
constraint $\Pr\{\tilde C(n,v)\le B\}\ge 1-\beta_c$, the carbon-side counterpart of the cycle service level
$\alpha$ on the inventory side.

\begin{proposition}[Deterministic equivalent of the chance constraint]\label{prop:chance}
This chance constraint is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chancecap}
  \varphi n + \eta\,\sstock(n,v) + m\big[\varepsilon(v)N\mu D+\varepsilon_L N\mu\, d(n)\big]\;\le\;B,
\end{equation}
the carbon function \eqref{eq:C} with the transport component scaled by $m=q_{1-\beta_c}/(N\mu)$, where
$q_{1-\beta_c}=\inf\{q:\Pr(Q\le q)\ge1-\beta_c\}$ is the lower $(1-\beta_c)$ quantile of realized throughput $Q$, and the facility and holding terms
unchanged. Under the normal approximation $Q\sim\mathcal N(N\mu,\sigma_Q^2)$ this is
$m=1+z_{\beta_c}\,\mathrm{CV}_Q$, with $z_{\beta_c}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\beta_c)$ and $\mathrm{CV}_Q=\sigma_Q/(N\mu)$; then
$m\ge 1$ whenever $\beta_c\le\tfrac12$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent The chance constraint therefore leaves the structure intact: \eqref{eq:chancecap} is the
four-component carbon function with the inbound and last-mile carbon rates scaled, $\varepsilon_0\mapsto
m\varepsilon_0$ and $\varepsilon_L\mapsto m\varepsilon_L$, while cost and the facility and holding terms are
unchanged. Because $Q$ is realized market throughput, exogenous to the firm's choice of $n$ and $v$, the
factor $m$ is a decision-invariant scalar on the transport carbon rates; the priced objective is again
\eqref{eq:J} under this substitution, so Propositions~\ref{prop:unique}--\ref{prop:path} carry over with
$\varepsilon_0$ and $\varepsilon_L$ replaced by $m\varepsilon_0$ and $m\varepsilon_L$, and only the thresholds
shift.

\begin{corollary}[Uncertainty tightens the speed threshold and relaxes the density threshold]\label{cor:chance}
Under the chance-constrained cap the speed threshold \eqref{eq:speedthreshold} becomes
$a(n,v_{\mathrm K})\,\eta/h>m\,\varepsilon'(v_{\mathrm K})/t'(v_{\mathrm K})$ ($m\,\varepsilon_0/t_1$ under the
quadratic), and the
pooling-adjusted density threshold \eqref{eq:densthreshpool} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chancedensthresh}
  m\,\frac{\varepsilon_L}{t_L} \;>\; \frac{\varphi+\eta\,G\big(n_K(v),v\big)}{F+h\,G\big(n_K(v),v\big)},
\end{equation}
which reduces to $m\,\varepsilon_L/t_L>\varphi/F$ in the no-pooling limit ($\rho\to1$); in the joint design
the speed ratio becomes $a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}/m$ and the density condition \eqref{eq:jointdens} becomes
\[
  m\,r_L>(1-\omega)\,r_F+\omega\,m^{1/5}r_h^{4/5}r_t^{1/5}a_K^{-1/5}.
\] Under the normal approximation, $m\ge 1$ for
$\beta_c\le\tfrac12$, with equality at $\beta_c=\tfrac12$, so the chance constraint tightens the speed-reversal
threshold and relaxes the dispersion threshold relative to the expected-value cap, and the effect grows as $m$ rises with the demand
coefficient of variation $\sigma/\mu$, the correlation $\rho$, and the confidence $1-\beta_c$. Under the quadratic instantiation, in the joint design the carbon-minimizing speed is nonincreasing in $m$,
and strictly decreasing where $n_C$ and
$v_C$ are interior.
\end{corollary}

\noindent Demand uncertainty therefore affects carbon through two channels. It enters the inventory-holding component through the safety stock
$z\sigma\sqrt{L}$, the channel behind the speed reversal, and, under a chance-constrained cap, it enters the
transport component through the margin $z_{\beta_c}\,\mathrm{CV}_Q$ that the firm holds against realized emissions
just as it holds safety stock against demand. More variable or more correlated demand raises this margin and,
like a higher service requirement, shifts the carbon-minimizing configuration; the expected-value cap is the
limiting case $m=1$. Carrying the realized variability of on-hand inventory as well would place an analogous
margin on the inventory-holding component; we keep the safety stock at its planned level, consistent with the base-stock
policy. The deterministic equivalent itself is distribution-free in the throughput quantile $q_{1-\beta_c}$;
the normal approximation enters only through the closed form $m=1+z_{\beta_c}\,\mathrm{CV}_Q$, which is most
accurate for moderate $N$ and modest $\mathrm{CV}_Q$ and which a firm with strongly skewed demand can replace
by the empirical throughput quantile without altering the structure.

The cap applies over the same accounting period as the flow model --- here a week. For a cap on cumulative
carbon over $T$ periods with demands independent across periods, aggregate throughput has coefficient of
variation $\mathrm{CV}_Q/\sqrt{T}$, so the multiplier tightens toward one as the horizon lengthens: in the
calibrated network of Section~\ref{sec:numerical}, the $95\%$ multiplier falls from $m=1.37$ for a weekly cap to $m=1.05$ for an annual
cap ($T=52$). The chance-constrained results are therefore most relevant to high-frequency operational
carbon budgets, shipment-cycle accounting, and corporate carbon controls, and less binding for annual
compliance accounting.



\paragraph{Load-factor sensitivity.} Our model treats the per-unit last-mile rates $t_L$ and $\varepsilon_L$
as independent of shipment size. Last-mile cost and carbon, however, accrue largely per \emph{vehicle}-mile
--- driver time and fuel --- so the per-unit rates scale with vehicle-miles per unit, which rise as
dispersion thins each territory's dispatch volume and average vehicle fill falls; load-sensitive rates therefore
introduce a consolidation force outside the constant-rate model. Because a fill multiplier depends on $n$,
it enters the marginal density condition through both the level and the derivative of the last-mile term.
When a common fill multiplier is applied to the cost and the carbon rate alike, both optima shift toward
consolidation, while their relative direction remains consistent with the
carbon-intensity comparison. Per vehicle-mile, cost is dominated by driver time while fuel use falls somewhat in an
emptier vehicle --- the basis of tonne-kilometer emission accounting \citep{glec2022framework} --- so when
fill deteriorates, the per-unit cost rate typically inflates at least as strongly as the per-unit carbon
rate. A multiplier falling more heavily on carbon is therefore a stress case for the dispersion result; under
it, the density comparison can shift toward consolidation. A full treatment would make shipment size, routing, and consolidation additional decisions; we leave it as an
extension.
\section{A Calibrated Application}\label{sec:numerical}

This section applies the results to distribution activities measured from public US data.
Section~\ref{sec:calib} constructs benchmark carbon intensities per dollar from public sources and stated
assumptions, Section~\ref{sec:network} traces how a carbon price changes the speed and density of a reference
network, and Section~\ref{sec:robust} examines whether that response survives changes in the assumptions. The
directions follow from the propositions once the ratios are known, subject to the bounds on speed; the
magnitudes describe the network we calibrate.

\subsection{Benchmark carbon intensities}\label{sec:calib}

Each ratio is the carbon an activity emits per dollar it costs. We compute it from the bottom up, as the
activity's energy use times the emission factor of that energy, divided by the activity's cost, and compare the
result with the industry factors of the US environmentally extended input--output model \citep{epa2024useeio}.
A dollar spent on energy carries about $2.8$~kg CO\textsubscript{2}e as diesel, $2.9$--$4.6$~kg as grid
electricity, and $5.0$--$5.8$~kg as marine fuel, while the labor and capital that make up the rest of logistics
cost carry little carbon of their own. An activity's carbon intensity per dollar is therefore close to its energy
share of cost times a few kilograms.

$\varphi$, from the increment that varies with the inventory held, $\eta$. We split building energy by a share

variable for an owned facility; we take $\beta=\gamma=\tfrac12$. The two shares divide one building's energy and
cost, so each is counted once. Storage emissions per pallet depend on the storage environment and not on the
value of the goods. We treat site energy as electricity, which overstates carbon for buildings partly heated by
gas. Table~\ref{tab:ratios} reports the ratios.\footnote{Sourced inputs. Emission factors: diesel $10.21$~kg

carbon-minimizing network has $23$ centers when faster service emits $0.03$~kg per dollar and $21$ when it emits

\section{Managerial Implications}\label{sec:managerial}

The results give a firm a diagnostic it can compute from its own rate data, and they indicate which of its
distribution decisions a carbon price or cap will move, in which direction, and by how much. Each implication below follows from a result of Section~\ref{sec:analysis}, and the calibrated values of
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} illustrate it.

\paragraph{A diagnostic in two questions.} A firm needs four ratios and one cost share. The ratios are the
carbon per dollar of holding stock, of the speed it can buy on its line-haul lanes, of last-mile delivery, and of
operating a distribution center. Each is close to the activity's energy share of cost times the carbon of a
dollar of its energy (Section~\ref{sec:calib}), so it can be estimated from fuel and electricity spend. The cost share is the pooling weight $\omega$ --- the share of a distribution center's marginal cost that is lost
pooling --- and the firm can obtain it from its cost-minimizing network through \eqref{eq:poolweight}.
Proposition~\ref{prop:classify} then answers two questions: whether replenishment should be faster or slower,
and whether the network should have more or fewer distribution centers. Table~\ref{tab:regimes} sets out the four
answers.

\paragraph{Slow the speed that is bought with fuel.} A faster vessel, truck instead of intermodal rail, and air
instead of truck add cost that is mostly fuel, so their carbon per dollar is many times that of holding stock.
Proposition~\ref{prop:speed} then calls for slower replenishment, and in our calibration the carbon pressure points that way for every product we examine, perishables included; it
changes speed only where the cost-minimizing chain buys some speed (Table~\ref{tab:network}). The shifts of pharmaceutical
freight from air to ocean and of truckload lanes to intermodal rail are consistent with this direction
\citep{pharmcommerce2023astrazeneca, freightwaves2026intermodal}, as is the finding of \citet{maloni2013slow}
that slower vessel speed lowers carbon and total cost despite the added pipeline inventory. Where the cost-minimizing chain already ships at
the slowest option, as it does for ambient goods in Section~\ref{sec:network}, a carbon price leaves speed there.

\paragraph{Buy speed with labor where speed pays.} Speed bought with labor, such as a team of drivers on the same
tractor, adds wages rather than fuel, so its carbon per dollar is close to zero and a carbon price favors it
(Proposition~\ref{prop:speed}). It matters where holding is costly enough for speed to pay at all: in
Section~\ref{sec:network}, carbon moves the labor lane toward team operation for dairy whose spoilage carries the carbon of its production,
leaves ambient goods with a solo driver, and slows high-value goods, whose holding carbon per dollar is small. Demand volatility and the service target raise the cost-minimizing and the
carbon-minimizing speeds together, so they change how fast the chain runs but not the direction in which carbon
moves it.

\paragraph{Let the last-mile vehicle set the number of distribution centers.} The density decision compares the
carbon per dollar of last-mile delivery with that of a distribution center's facility and lost pooling
(Proposition~\ref{prop:classify}). Heavy diesel delivery is carbon-intensive enough to call for more
distribution centers closer to demand, several times as many at the carbon minimum of Section~\ref{sec:network}. Electric vans call for fewer, while
diesel vans and electric trucks are close to the threshold, where facility energy and the pooling weight decide. Amazon reports that placing inventory closer to
customers avoided $127$ million vehicle miles in 2025 \citep{amazon2025sustainability}, a move in the direction
Proposition~\ref{prop:classify} gives when the last mile is carbon-intensive. A firm that electrifies its last mile should therefore revisit how many distribution centers it operates. A firm
that adds centers to shorten delivery should weigh the vehicle fill it gives up, since emptier vehicles raise both the cost and the carbon of each
delivered unit (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).

\paragraph{Plan line haul and network together.} Because faster replenishment recovers part of the pooling that
dispersion gives up, the carbon intensity of line-haul freight enters the density condition
(Proposition~\ref{prop:classify}): a lower-emission line haul lowers the threshold for adding distribution
centers. The effect works through the pooling weight. It is small for networks whose fixed costs are large
relative to their safety stock, as in Section~\ref{sec:network}, and larger for volatile products and high service
targets, for which lost pooling is a larger share of a center's marginal cost (Proposition~\ref{prop:service}). More
generally, the two decisions are complements (Proposition~\ref{prop:complement}), so a program that acts on one
alone, such as a slow-steaming mandate or a consolidation drive, evaluates it at a level of the other decision
that the joint design would change.

\paragraph{Expect modest moves at current carbon prices.} Proposition~\ref{prop:path} ties the response to a
carbon price to the share of carbon in each activity's priced cost. At $\$100$ per tonne, carbon is under $1\%$ of the priced cost of holding and of facilities, $5\%$ of diesel truck delivery, $15\%$ of speed bought with
truck instead of rail, and $35\%$ of speed bought with vessel speed; it reaches half of the priced cost of diesel
truck delivery only at about $\$2{,}000$ per tonne. A carbon price in the range now in use therefore moves the
design little, by less than one percent of network carbon for ambient goods in Section~\ref{sec:network}. It moves fuel-bought
speed first, consistent with the attention slow steaming has received as a carbon
response in shipping \citep{psaraftis2013speed}. Larger redesigns follow from caps that bind: at the carbon
minimum the network of Section~\ref{sec:network} has several times as many distribution centers. Without
pooling, the carbon that a cost-minimizing design leaves unabated grows, to second order, with the square of the
logarithm of each ratio (Corollary~\ref{cor:nopool}), so redesign pays most for products whose ratios lie far
from one.

\paragraph{Account for realized carbon over short horizons.} A cap on realized rather than expected carbon places
a risk margin on transport emissions (Proposition~\ref{prop:chance}). For a weekly cap held with $95\%$
confidence, the margin raises the transport carbon rates by $37\%$ in our calibration and so reinforces slower fuel-bought
speed and, with diesel delivery, more distribution centers (Corollary~\ref{cor:chance}). For an annual cap the
margin is about $5\%$ and changes little.

\paragraph{Implications for policy.} An instrument that prices or caps transport alone, such as a freight fuel
levy or a vehicle emissions standard, blends the carbon price into the transport rates and leaves holding and
facility carbon unpriced. At a given density it therefore slows replenishment, and at a given speed it adds
distribution centers, whatever the product. By the marginal comparisons of Propositions~\ref{prop:speed}
and~\ref{prop:density}, these moves lower total carbon when they agree with the carbon-minimizing direction and,
for small prices, raise it when they do not. A transport-only price therefore raises total carbon at the cost optimum when
holding is more carbon-intensive per dollar than the speed it would buy, or when the last mile is less
carbon-intensive per dollar than facilities and lost pooling. In our calibration the first case arises when speed
bought with labor is in use and the second with electric last-mile vans. An instrument that prices total
logistics carbon, including warehousing, lets the firm choose the speed and density that minimize carbon across
all four activities.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

A firm that distributes through regional distribution centers provides availability through safety stock or
replenishment speed and proximity through facilities or last-mile travel, and a carbon price or cap can change
which activity provides each. We have shown that the joint design has a single best solution at every carbon price and that the
carbon-minimizing direction of both decisions follows from four carbon-intensity-per-dollar ratios and the share
of a distribution center's marginal cost that is lost pooling. The carbon intensity of line-haul freight enters
the density decision through the speed that recovers lost pooling. In public US data, the classification depends
less on the product than on how speed is bought and how the last mile is driven. Speed bought with fuel is slowed
and speed bought with labor is favored, while heavy diesel delivery
calls for more distribution centers and electric vans for fewer. Because carbon is a small share of each
activity's priced cost at current prices, a carbon price moves the design little, whereas a cap that binds can
change it substantially. Several documented moves in practice are consistent with these directions; in the sources we examine, firms
report them in terms of cost, delivery service, and transport miles. Our model adds the carbon of the inventory a
slower chain holds and the link between speed and density.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The model describes an average, symmetric distribution environment, so its
classification is a diagnostic for a heterogeneous network rather than a design for it. Heterogeneous markets or
discrete facility sites replace $d(n)=\delta n^{-1/2}$ with the realized last-mile profile; this can move the
density threshold but leaves the comparison between facility and last-mile carbon per dollar intact. The representative inbound distance isolates the proximity effect of adding distribution centers. If more centers also
mean smaller inbound shipments, lower vehicle fill, or lost facility scale, those forces favor consolidation and
can move a case near the threshold, as last-mile fill does (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}). We treat speed as a continuous index between real options on a lane. A firm that splits volume between modes
faces a mixture of lead times, and our safety-stock expression does not represent such a mixture. The model ties last-mile distance to the number of
stocking distribution centers, whereas networks that deliver through cross-docks or hubs separate stocking density
from delivery density. Service enters as a cycle-service requirement; if it is chosen, safety stock becomes a
further design margin. Finally, the carbon intensities are calibrated to public industry data rather than to any
firm's records, so the classification should be computed with a firm's own rates, and the magnitudes in
Section~\ref{sec:numerical} describe the network we calibrate.

\paragraph{Future research.} Multi-product and multi-echelon settings would show how a shared network serves
products in different regimes and where safety stock should be held. Separating stocking from delivery density,
and modeling shipment consolidation and routing explicitly, would quantify the centralizing forces represented
here by fill sensitivity. Accounting under ISO 14083 makes firm-level logistics-emissions data increasingly available
\citep{iso2023_14083}; such data would locate real products relative to the speed and density thresholds.
Endogenous service choice and explicit mode mixes would make the service target and a lane's mode split design
decisions rather than inputs.

Carbon accounting asks a distribution network two questions, how fast to replenish and how many distribution
centers to operate, and the answer depends on which activity provides availability and proximity at the lower
carbon intensity per dollar. In the US data we calibrate, that comparison favors slower fuel-bought freight for
every product and, where the last mile runs on heavy diesel vehicles, more distribution centers closer to demand.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:blend}]
The blended rates in \eqref{eq:J} follow by collecting coefficients in $K+\tau C$; the financing term has no
carbon counterpart. The speed comparison sets holding, weighted by the share $a$, against inbound transport at the
margin (Proposition~\ref{prop:speed}); the density comparison sets
last-mile transport against a pooling-adjusted blend of facilities and holding
(Proposition~\ref{prop:density}). The four ratios listed in the statement are therefore the basic carbon-intensity comparisons used below.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:unique}]
We give the argument for $J$; for $C$ replace each blended rate by its carbon rate and set $h_p=0$. Let $x=\ln n$ and $y=\ln v$,
which range over a box, and let $k=z\sigma\sqrt{ND}$. Then
\[
  J=F_\tau e^{x}+H_\tau k\,e^{g(x,y)}+h_pN\mu D\,e^{-y}+T_\tau(e^{y})\,N\mu D+\ell_\tau N\mu\delta\,e^{-x/2},
\]
with $g(x,y)=\tfrac12\ln\big(N\rho+(1-\rho)e^{x}\big)-\tfrac12y$.
(i) The facility and last-mile terms are strictly convex in $x$. The function $g$ is convex, because
$\ln(c_1+c_2e^{x})$ is convex for $c_1\ge0$ and $c_2>0$, so the holding term is log-convex and therefore convex. The
transport term is convex in $y$, because $T_\tau$ is convex and nondecreasing and $e^{y}$ is convex. For strict
convexity, let $S=H_\tau k\,e^{g}$; its Hessian is $S\big(\nabla^2g+\nabla g\,\nabla g^{\mathsf T}\big)$, with
$\nabla g=(g_x,-\tfrac12)$ and $\nabla^2g=\mathrm{diag}(g_{xx},0)$, $g_{xx}\ge0$. The Hessian of the facility,
holding, and last-mile terms therefore has $(x,x)$ entry
$F_\tau e^{x}+\tfrac14\ell_\tau N\mu\delta e^{-x/2}+S(g_{xx}+g_x^2)>0$, $(y,y)$ entry $S/4$, and off-diagonal
entry $-Sg_x/2$, so its determinant is $\big(F_\tau e^{x}+\tfrac14\ell_\tau N\mu\delta e^{-x/2}\big)S/4+S^2g_{xx}/4>0$.
These terms are strictly convex, and adding the convex transport and financing terms preserves strict
convexity; the financing term $h_pN\mu D\,e^{-y}$ is convex in $y$ and does not depend on $x$.
(ii) A strictly convex continuous function has a unique minimizer on a compact convex set. Since $J$ is continuous
in $(x,y,\tau)$, the minimizer is continuous in $\tau$ by the maximum theorem.
(iii) For fixed $x$, $J$ is strictly convex in $y$, so the speed response is unique and, by the maximum theorem,
continuous. The transport, financing, facility, and last-mile terms are additively separable in $x$ and $y$, and the
cross-partial of the holding term is $-Sg_x/2\le0$, so $J$ has decreasing differences in $(x,y)$ and the minimizing
$y$ is nondecreasing in $x$. At a fixed speed, $J$ is strictly convex in $x$; the reduced objective is the partial
minimum of a strictly convex function over a convex set and is therefore strictly convex in $x$. A strictly convex
function of $\ln n$ is strictly quasiconvex in $n$. By Danskin's theorem, which applies because the speed response
is unique, $J^{\mathrm r}$ is differentiable with $J^{\mathrm r}_n(n)=J_n\big(n,v^*(n)\big)$, and by strict
quasiconvexity $J^{\mathrm r}_n$ changes sign at most once, from negative to positive.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:speed}]
$N\mu D\,t(v)$, $N\mu D\,\varepsilon(v)$, and $h_pN\mu D\,v^{-1}$ are convex and $\kappa(n)v^{-1/2}$ has
$\partial_v^2=\tfrac34 \kappa(n)v^{-5/2}>0$, so both objectives are strictly convex in $v$. We use
$M_s=\tfrac12 h\,\kappa(n)\,v_{\mathrm K}^{-3/2}=h\sstock/(2v_{\mathrm K})$ and
$M_p=h_pN\mu D\,v_{\mathrm K}^{-2}=h_pP/v_{\mathrm K}$ to denote the marginal savings of safety stock and of stock
in transit, so that $a(n,v_{\mathrm K})=M_s/(M_s+M_p)$. The cost optimum
satisfies $\hat K'(v_{\mathrm K})=0$, i.e.\ $N\mu D\,t'(v_{\mathrm K})=M_s+M_p$, so that
$\tfrac12 \kappa(n)v_{\mathrm K}^{-3/2}=a(n,v_{\mathrm K})\,N\mu D\,t'(v_{\mathrm K})/h$. Evaluating the carbon
derivative there,
\[
  \hat C'(v_{\mathrm K})=-\eta\,\tfrac12 \kappa(n)v_{\mathrm K}^{-3/2}+N\mu D\,\varepsilon'(v_{\mathrm K})
  =N\mu D\big(\varepsilon'(v_{\mathrm K})-a(n,v_{\mathrm K})\,(\eta/h)\,t'(v_{\mathrm K})\big),
\]
which is negative --- so $v_C>v_{\mathrm K}$ by strict convexity --- if and only if \eqref{eq:speedthreshold}.
With $t'=2t_1 v$ and $\varepsilon'=2\varepsilon_0 v$ the marginal ratio is $\varepsilon_0/t_1$, and with $h_p=0$
the priced first-order condition \eqref{eq:focv} gives
$v^{5/2}=(h+\tau\eta)z\sigma\sqrt{ND}\,\sqrt{\Psi}/[4(t_1+\tau\varepsilon_0)N\mu D]$, which is
\eqref{eq:vstar}. The projection and the boundary qualification are immediate.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:service}]
Let $s=z\sigma$, so that $\sstock\propto s\,v^{-1/2}$. Condition \eqref{eq:focv} takes the form $M_s+M_p=2(t_1+\tau\varepsilon_0)N\mu D\,v$
with $M_s=H_\tau\sstock/(2v)\propto s\,v^{-3/2}$ and $M_p=h_pN\mu D\,v^{-2}$, and let $a_\tau=M_s/(M_s+M_p)$. Implicit
differentiation gives $\mathrm d\ln v^*/\mathrm d\ln s=M_s/(\tfrac52M_s+3M_p)=a_\tau/(3-a_\tau/2)\in(0,\tfrac25]$, so
speed rises with service and demand volatility; without financing $v^*\propto s^{2/5}$, as in \eqref{eq:vstar}.
Along this speed response, $J^{\mathrm r}_n(n;s)=J_n\big(n,v^*(n;s);s\big)$ because $J_v=0$. The holding marginal
term in \eqref{eq:focn} is proportional to $s(v^*)^{-1/2}(1-\rho)/\sqrt{\Psi(n)}$, whose elasticity in $s$ is
$1-a_\tau/(6-a_\tau)\ge\tfrac45$, hence it rises in $s$ even after the induced speed increase. Thus $J^{\mathrm r}_{ns}>0$. By Proposition~\ref{prop:unique}(iii), $J^{\mathrm r}_n(\cdot\,;s)$ changes sign at most once, from negative to
positive, and it is increasing in $s$; its sign change therefore occurs at a lower $n$ when $s$ rises, so the
optimal density is nonincreasing in $s$, and strictly decreasing where it is interior. Finally, at $\tau=0$,
$M_s/M_p\propto s\,v_{\mathrm K}^{1/2}$ rises in $s$, directly and through $v_{\mathrm K}$, so $a(n,v_{\mathrm K})$
rises in $s$; under the quadratic instantiation the right side of \eqref{eq:speedthreshold} is the constant
$\varepsilon_0/t_1$, so the comparison can change only toward faster replenishment.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:density}]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:unique}(iii), $J(\cdot,v)$ and $C(\cdot,v)$ are strictly quasiconvex on $[1,N]$, which
gives uniqueness and the interior/boundary characterization. For the threshold, take $\tau=0$ for cost and
$\tau\to\infty$ for carbon. The cost density satisfies $\partial_n K=F+h\,G(n_K,v)-
t_L N\mu\delta/(2n_K^{3/2})=0$, so the last-mile marginal $t_L N\mu\delta/(2n_K^{3/2})=F+h\,G(n_K,v)$.
Evaluating the carbon derivative there,
\[
  \partial_n C\big|_{n_K}
  =\varphi+\eta\,G(n_K,v)-\frac{\varepsilon_L N\mu\delta}{2n_K^{3/2}}
  =\varphi+\eta\,G(n_K,v)-\frac{\varepsilon_L}{t_L}\,\big[F+h\,G(n_K,v)\big],
\]
which is negative --- so $n_C>n_K$ by strict quasiconvexity of $C(\cdot,v)$ --- if and only if
\eqref{eq:densthreshpool}. With $R(G)=(\varphi+\eta G)/(F+hG)$, we have $R(0)=\varphi/F$, $R(G)\to\eta/h$ as $G\to\infty$, and
$R'(G)=(\eta F-h\varphi)/(F+hG)^2$, so $R$ is monotone with $\operatorname{sign}R'=\operatorname{sign}
(\eta/h-\varphi/F)$ and lies between $\varphi/F$ and $\eta/h$. In the no-pooling limit $\rho\to1$, $G\to0$ and $\Psi\to N$, so
\eqref{eq:focn} gives $n^*=(\ell_\tau N\mu\delta/2F_\tau)^{2/3}$ and \eqref{eq:densthreshpool} becomes \eqref{eq:densitythreshold}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:complement}]
Since $G(n,v)\propto v^{-1/2}$, the cross-partial is $J_{nv}=H_\tau G_v$, which equals
$-H_\tau G(n,v)/(2v)<0$ for $\rho<1$. For the speed response, where $v^*$ is interior \eqref{eq:focv} holds; its right side strictly
increases in $n$, through $\sstock$, and strictly decreases in $v$, while its left side does not depend on $n$ and is nondecreasing
in $v$, so $v^*$ strictly increases in $n$, and projection onto the speed bounds preserves monotonicity. For the density response, $J_n(\cdot,v)$ changes sign at most once, from negative to positive
(Proposition~\ref{prop:unique}(iii)), and $J_{nv}<0$; its sign change therefore occurs at a higher $n$ when $v$
rises.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:classify}]
(i) By Proposition~\ref{prop:unique} applied to the carbon objective, $n_C>n_K$ if and only if the reduced
carbon objective slopes downward at $n_K$, $C^{\mathrm r}_n(n_K)<0$, and $n_C=n_K$ if it is zero. By the
envelope theorem (Danskin's theorem, since $\hat v_C$ is unique whether or not it is at a bound),
$C^{\mathrm r}_n(n_K)=\varphi+\eta\,G(n_K,\hat v_C)-\tfrac12\varepsilon_LN\mu\delta\,n_K^{-3/2}$. The financing
term does not depend on $n$, so the cost condition \eqref{eq:focn} at $\tau=0$, which holds because $n_K$ is
interior, gives $\tfrac12t_LN\mu\delta\,n_K^{-3/2}=F+hG(n_K,v_K)$, and the last term equals
$r_L\big[F+hG(n_K,v_K)\big]$. Since $G\propto v^{-1/2}$, $\eta\,G(n_K,\hat v_C)=h\,G(n_K,v_K)\,r_h\sqrt{v_K/\hat v_C}$.
Dividing $C^{\mathrm r}_n(n_K)$ by $F+hG(n_K,v_K)>0$ and substituting $\varphi=F\,r_F$ gives \eqref{eq:jointdensgen}.
Under the quadratic instantiation with $v_K$ and $\hat v_C$ interior, the carbon condition \eqref{eq:focv} at
$n_K$ gives $\eta\sstock(n_K,\hat v_C)=4\varepsilon_0N\mu D\,\hat v_C^2$, and the cost condition gives
$h\sstock(n_K,v_K)=4a_Kt_1N\mu D\,v_K^2$; with $\sstock\propto v^{-1/2}$ these imply
$(\hat v_C/v_K)^{5/2}=a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}$, so $r_h\sqrt{v_K/\hat v_C}=r_h^{4/5}r_t^{1/5}a_K^{-1/5}$, which is
\eqref{eq:jointdens}.
The second expression for $\omega$ in \eqref{eq:poolweight} follows from $F+hG(n_K,v_K)=\tfrac12t_LN\mu\delta\,
n_K^{-3/2}$ and $F=\tfrac12t_LN\mu\delta\,(n_K^0)^{-3/2}$.
(ii) The carbon objective has no financing term, so by \eqref{eq:focv} its speed response satisfies
$v_C(n)^{5/2}\propto\sqrt{\Psi(n)}$, and $\ln[v_C/\hat v_C]=\tfrac15\ln[\Psi(n_C)/\Psi(n_K)]$. Adding
$\ln[\hat v_C/v_K]=\tfrac25\ln(a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}})$ from part (i) gives \eqref{eq:speeddecomp}.
(iii) If \eqref{eq:jointdens} holds, then $n_C>n_K$ and $\Psi(n_C)>\Psi(n_K)$, so in \eqref{eq:speeddecomp} the
first term is nonnegative and the second positive; the reversed case is symmetric. Since $\Psi$ is increasing
with $\Psi(1)\le\Psi(n_C)\le\Psi(N)=N$, the threshold $\sqrt{\Psi(n_K)/\Psi(n_C)}$ in (ii) lies in
$[\underline R,\overline R]$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:nopool}]
With $\rho=1$, $\Psi\equiv N$ and $G\equiv0$, so \eqref{eq:focn} involves only $F_\tau$ and $\ell_\tau$ and
gives $n^*(\tau)=(\ell_\tau N\mu\delta/2F_\tau)^{2/3}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:density}); the ratio of its
carbon and cost values is $(r_L/r_F)^{2/3}$; the financing term does not depend on $n$. The speed response no
longer depends on $n$, and by the argument of Proposition~\ref{prop:classify}(i) the ratio of its carbon and cost
values is $(a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}})^{2/5}$. For \eqref{eq:excess}, a function
$c_1n+c_2n^{-1/2}$ attains its minimum $3c_1n^\circ$ at $n^\circ=(c_2/2c_1)^{2/3}$ and equals
$c_1n^\circ(x+2x^{-1/2})$ at $n=xn^\circ$; with $n_K=xn_C$, $C_{\mathrm{prox}}(n_K)/C_{\mathrm{prox}}(n_C)=(x+2x^{-1/2})/3$.
Likewise $c_1v^{-1/2}+c_2v^2$ attains its minimum $\tfrac54c_1(v^\circ)^{-1/2}$ and equals
$c_1(v^\circ)^{-1/2}(y^{-1/2}+y^2/4)$ at $v=yv^\circ$, so with $v_K=yv_C$,
$C_{\mathrm{avail}}(v_K)/C_{\mathrm{avail}}(v_C)=(4y^{-1/2}+y^2)/5$. Weighting by $\theta$ and $1-\theta$ gives \eqref{eq:excess};
expanding $x=e^{u_1}$ and $y=e^{u_2}$ to second order gives the last statement.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:path}]
(i) Condition \eqref{eq:focv} is $\Upsilon(v,\tau)=2b_\tau N\mu D\,v-M_s-M_p=0$, with $b_\tau=t_1+\tau\varepsilon_0$,
$M_s=H_\tau\sstock/(2v)$, and $M_p=h_pP/v$. Then $v\,\Upsilon_v=\tfrac52M_s+3M_p$ and, since
$\tau\eta/H_\tau=s_h$ and $\tau\varepsilon_0/b_\tau=s_t$, $\tau\,\Upsilon_\tau=s_t(M_s+M_p)-s_hM_s$. Hence
$\mathrm d\ln v^*/\mathrm d\ln\tau=-\tau\Upsilon_\tau/(v\Upsilon_v)=(a_\tau s_h-s_t)/(3-a_\tau/2)$ with $a_\tau=M_s/(M_s+M_p)$.
Substituting the definitions, $a_\tau s_h-s_t=\tau\,(h\sstock+2h_pP)\,t_1\,[a(n,v^*)\,r_h-r_t]/
[(H_\tau\sstock+2h_pP)\,b_\tau]$, which has the sign of $a(n,v^*)r_h-r_t$. Because $a(n,v)$ rises with $v$, a
positive sign raises $v^*$ and keeps the sign positive, and a negative sign lowers $v^*$ and keeps it negative, so
the sign does not change along the path and $v^*$ is monotone in $\tau$. With $h_p=0$, $a_\tau=1$ and the
elasticity is $\tfrac25(s_h-s_t)$. (ii) The same computation
applies to $n^*=(\ell_\tau N\mu\delta/2F_\tau)^{2/3}$. (iii) With $h_p=0$, by \eqref{eq:vstar}, along the speed response
$H_\tau G\big(n,v^*(n)\big)=c\,(1-\rho)\,H_\tau^{4/5}b_\tau^{1/5}\Psi(n)^{-3/5}$, with $b_\tau=t_1+\tau\varepsilon_0$
and $c>0$ depending on neither $n$ nor $\tau$. By the envelope theorem, $J^{\mathrm r}_n=F_\tau+H_\tau G\big(n,v^*(n)\big)-\tfrac12\ell_\tau N\mu\delta n^{-3/2}$;
multiplying by $n^{3/2}/\ell_\tau>0$ preserves its sign,
\[
  \frac{n^{3/2}J^{\mathrm r}_n}{\ell_\tau}=\frac{F_\tau}{\ell_\tau}\,n^{3/2}
  +c\,(1-\rho)\,\frac{H_\tau^{4/5}b_\tau^{1/5}}{\ell_\tau}\,\frac{n^{3/2}}{\Psi(n)^{3/5}}-\tfrac12N\mu\delta .
\]
This expression changes sign at most once, from negative to positive (Proposition~\ref{prop:unique}(iii)). If
both coefficients are nonincreasing in $\tau$, the expression is pointwise nonincreasing in $\tau$, so its sign
change moves weakly to higher $n$ and $n^*(\tau)$ is nondecreasing. The logarithmic derivatives of
the coefficients are $1/(r_F^{-1}+\tau)-1/(r_L^{-1}+\tau)$ and
$\tfrac45/(r_h^{-1}+\tau)+\tfrac15/(r_t^{-1}+\tau)-1/(r_L^{-1}+\tau)$. Since $1/(r^{-1}+\tau)$ is increasing in
$r$, both are nonpositive at every $\tau$ when $r_L$ exceeds $r_F$, $r_h$, and $r_t$, and nonnegative when it
falls short of each.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:chance}]
In \eqref{eq:Ctilde} the throughput coefficient $\gamma(n,v)=\varepsilon(v)D+\varepsilon_L d(n)>0$, so
$\{\tilde C\le B\}=\{Q\le(B-\varphi n-\eta\sstock)/\gamma\}$, and $\Pr\{\tilde C\le B\}\ge 1-\beta_c$ holds iff
$(B-\varphi n-\eta\sstock)/\gamma\ge q_{1-\beta_c}$, i.e.\ $\varphi n+\eta\sstock+\gamma\,q_{1-\beta_c}\le B$. Because
$\gamma\,q_{1-\beta_c}=(q_{1-\beta_c}/N\mu)\,[\varepsilon(v)N\mu D+\varepsilon_L N\mu d(n)]$, this condition is
\eqref{eq:chancecap}.
Under normality $q_{1-\beta_c}=N\mu+z_{\beta_c}\sigma_Q$, so $m=1+z_{\beta_c}\,\mathrm{CV}_Q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:chance}]
The chance constraint multiplies the inbound and last-mile carbon rates by $m$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:chance}); substituting $m\varepsilon_0$ and $m\varepsilon_L$ for $\varepsilon_0$ and
$\varepsilon_L$ in \eqref{eq:speedthreshold} and \eqref{eq:densthreshpool} gives the two conditions, the latter
scaling only the left side and leaving the pooling-adjusted facility threshold $(\varphi+\eta G)/(F+hG)$ on
the right. The same substitution in Proposition~\ref{prop:classify}, with $n_K$, $\omega$, and $a_K$ unchanged because
the cost rates are unchanged, gives the joint conditions. From $\sigma_Q^2=\sigma^2N[1+(N-1)\rho]$, $\mathrm{CV}_Q=(\sigma/\mu)\sqrt{[1+(N-1)\rho]/N}$ is
increasing in $\sigma/\mu$ and, for $N>1$, in $\rho$, and $z_{\beta_c}$ is increasing in $1-\beta_c$, so
$m=1+z_{\beta_c}\mathrm{CV}_Q$ is increasing in all three. For the joint speed, divide the carbon density
condition by $m$: its facility and lost-pooling terms then scale as $m^{-1}$ and $m^{-4/5}$ and, multiplied by
$n^{3/2}$, rise in $n$ with elasticities $\tfrac32$ and at least $\tfrac9{10}$, so that, at an interior $n_C$,
$\mathrm d\ln n_C/\mathrm d\ln m\le\tfrac89$. Since $\Psi$ has elasticity at most one in $n$,
\eqref{eq:speeddecomp} with $a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}/m$ in place of $a_KR_{\mathrm{speed}}$ gives
$\mathrm d\ln v_C/\mathrm d\ln m\le-\tfrac25+\tfrac15\cdot\tfrac89<0$.
\end{proof}
