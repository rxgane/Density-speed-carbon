\begin{abstract}
\noindent We develop a quantitative decision model for carbon-induced operational substitution in distribution
network design. A firm distributes a single product from one source through a network of distribution centers
to many markets and chooses replenishment speed and network density to minimize cost subject to a carbon cap
and a service requirement. Distribution carbon arises from moving goods, operating facilities, and holding
inventory. The model treats availability and proximity as services that can each be provided by two activities:
availability through safety stock or replenishment speed, and proximity through facilities or last-mile travel.
Carbon accounting can select a different activity than cost minimization because each substitution reduces one
source of emissions while increasing another. Slower replenishment reduces freight emissions but increases
safety stock; consolidation reduces facility carbon and improves risk pooling but lengthens the last mile. We
derive closed-form thresholds that identify the carbon-minimizing direction of each decision for a given
product. The speed threshold compares holding carbon per dollar with inbound-transport carbon per dollar. The
density threshold compares last-mile carbon per dollar with a pooling-adjusted combination of facility and
holding intensities. Speed and density are complements, so the final configuration follows from the joint
decision. A chance-constrained cap on realized carbon shifts both thresholds through a throughput-risk
multiplier. Numerical illustrations show how the thresholds classify product archetypes and support
activity-level carbon accounting.
\end{abstract}

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Logistics; Distribution network design; Carbon emissions; Safety stock; Replenishment speed

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A firm serves its markets from a network of distribution centers that it replenishes over time. Among the operational choices this involves, we study two: the \emph{density} of the network --- how many distribution centers the firm operates --- and the \emph{speed} with which those centers are replenished. Both shape the firm's cost, and both shape the carbon its distribution emits. As carbon is increasingly priced and reported \citep{mckinnon2012setting}, a firm sets both with carbon as well as cost in view, and we study how a carbon price shapes that choice.

Each decision carries a standard cost--carbon trade-off. Network density governs proximity to markets: operating more distribution centers shortens the last-mile distance and changes the warehouse energy the network consumes. Replenishment speed governs availability: replenishing faster shortens the lead time that inventory must cover and changes the emissions of the freight that serves the network. Distribution emits carbon both when goods move and when they are held, and each affects both the firm's cost and its carbon. Cost balances these effects one way; with a price on carbon, the carbon-minimizing balance can fall elsewhere, and how it does so is the central question this paper addresses.

Slowing replenishment and consolidating the network are the common transport-side responses: slowing moves
freight by slower, lower-emission modes, as in slow steaming in shipping
\citep{psaraftis2013speed, cariou2011slow}, and consolidating serves markets from fewer, larger distribution
centers \citep{mckinnon2012setting}. Each response also affects inventory. A unit held in a warehouse consumes
energy --- refrigeration, climate control, handling --- for as long as it is held. Slowing replenishment
lengthens the lead time that a stock buffer must cover, so the safety stock, and the carbon of holding it,
rises with the square root of that lead time; consolidating pools demand across markets
\citep{eppen1979effects} and saves facility energy while lengthening the last mile. Each substitution thus lowers one
source of carbon and raises another, and whether it lowers the total depends on which source is larger.

The choice between the two sources is a choice between two operational substitutions. A firm
provides \emph{availability} either by holding safety stock or by replenishing faster --- a shorter lead
time leaves less for the buffer to cover, so speed and inventory are substitutes for the same service ---
and it provides market \emph{proximity} either by operating more local facilities or by delivering farther
from fewer, so facilities and last-mile travel are substitutes for the same access. The cost-minimizing configuration uses one mix of
these activities; when carbon is priced, the optimal mix can differ. When safety stock is more
carbon-intensive per dollar than the inbound transport that would replace it, the carbon-minimizing chain
provides availability through \emph{faster} replenishment. When last-mile travel is more carbon-intensive
per dollar than the facilities that would shorten it, the carbon-minimizing chain provides proximity through
facilities --- a \emph{more dispersed} network. The lowest-carbon distribution system thus depends on the
product: a cold-chain grocer and a bulk dry-goods shipper have different optimal configurations.

We formalize this in a single-product location--inventory model with stochastic demand. The firm chooses
network density and replenishment speed to minimize cost subject to a carbon cap and a service-level
requirement. Cost and carbon each comprise the same four components --- facilities, holding, inbound
transport, and last-mile transport. The model follows the location--inventory tradition
\citep{daskin2002inventory, shen2003joint} in a continuous-approximation form
\citep{daganzo2005logistics}, with a distinguishing addition: replenishment speed is a decision. Because
speed sets the lead time that stochastic safety stock must cover, it directly determines inventory
carbon and couples the four components together.

The paper contributes a threshold model for this joint decision. The organizing quantity is the
\emph{marginal carbon intensity per dollar} of each activity, and the model yields three structural results.
First, a \emph{speed reversal} in the availability substitution: holding
the network fixed, total carbon is U-shaped in speed, and the carbon-minimizing speed exceeds the
cost-minimizing one when holding is more carbon-intensive per dollar than inbound transport at the margin, so
a carbon constraint can raise the optimal replenishment speed --- a distinct configuration from the
slow-steaming literature. Second, a \emph{pooling-adjusted density threshold} in the proximity substitution. The
carbon-minimizing network is more dispersed when the last mile is carbon-intensive per dollar relative to
facilities. Adding a facility, however, also gives up some demand pooling, so the threshold is not that
facility comparison alone: it blends in the holding intensity, and therefore depends on demand correlation,
service, and volatility. Third, the two decisions are \emph{complements}: faster replenishment shrinks safety stock
and lowers the inventory penalty of dispersing, so the carbon-minimizing response adjusts both together rather
than one alone. Together, the two thresholds give a product-specific diagnostic for the joint
design problem: each compares the carbon intensity per dollar of the two competing activities and
identifies the carbon-minimizing direction --- faster or slower replenishment, a denser
or more dispersed network --- placing a product in one of four regimes. Scientifically, the thresholds
characterize when carbon accounting changes the operational activity selected by an optimal distribution
design. Practically, they require activity-level cost and carbon rates that firms can estimate from logistics
data. In one regime, both comparisons point to a network that is at once faster \emph{and} more dispersed. A
chance-constrained extension, in which the cap bounds realized rather than expected carbon, tightens the speed
threshold and relaxes the density one.

\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:related}

This study connects four streams: location--inventory design with risk pooling, carbon-aware network design,
the joint control of inventory and transport mode under emissions regulation, and carbon as a priced
operational cost in service-constrained inventory systems. Each develops an element of the two substitutions
examined here --- availability (inventory versus replenishment speed) and proximity (facilities versus
last-mile travel). Each of these streams is well developed, and we draw on all four; we position the contribution by what it adds
to each: the joint design of network density and replenishment speed under a carbon cap and a service
requirement, together with the closed-form thresholds that relate the carbon-minimizing configuration to the
cost-minimizing one.

\subsection{Location--inventory design and risk pooling}

A first stream establishes how demand risk pooling shapes distribution network design: jointly choosing
facility location and safety stock, it characterizes the cost-optimal number and placement of distribution
centers. \citet{eppen1979effects} characterizes the effect of centralization on expected cost in the
multi-location newsvendor, establishing the square-root relationship between the number of stocking locations
and pooled safety stock. \citet{daskin2002inventory} and \citet{shen2003joint} embed working and safety stock
into the facility-location decision, so that risk pooling shapes the optimal number and placement of
distribution centers, with extensions to stochastic settings by \citet{snyder2007stochastic}, to capacitated
warehouses by \citet{ozsen2008capacitated}, and to continuous-approximation allocation by
\citet{tsao2012continuous}. \citet{daganzo2005logistics} provides the continuous-approximation foundation in
which last-mile distance scales with the inverse square root of the number of facilities. Closest to our spatial--inventory
coupling, \citet{straubert2024continuous} ties the number of stocking locations to an exact
reorder-point/order-quantity inventory model through a delivery-lead-time penalty, solved by continuous
approximation. The centralize-versus-decentralize question these models pose can also turn on forces beyond
demand pooling: \citet{schmitt2015centralization} show analytically that supply-disruption risk diversification
can favor decentralization for a risk-averse firm, in contrast to the classical pooling result. These works
develop network density and safety-stock placement under risk pooling, with a cost or service objective and no
carbon; they do not make replenishment speed a continuous decision coupled with carbon. The present study
retains the risk-pooling structure and the density decision and adds an endogenous replenishment speed together
with a carbon objective.

\subsection{Carbon-aware network design}

A second stream brings carbon into facility location and network design, characterizing how an emissions
objective or cap can change the optimal network configuration. \citet{cachon2014retail}
relates retail store density to greenhouse gas cost, with consumer travel to stores as the dominant emission
channel and inventory entering through retail floor space. \citet{turken2017facility} examine facility location
and capacity under a carbon tax and emission limits, weighing centralization against decentralization through
production scale economies; \citet{harris2011assessing} compare cost-optimal and emissions-aware network
designs numerically; \citet{wang2020carbon} develop a carbon-compliant location--inventory model under
demand and carbon-price uncertainty; \citet{li2017joint} jointly optimize distribution-network design and
two-echelon inventory under a CO\textsubscript{2} emission tax; and \citet{hacardiaux2020assessing} assess the
transport-emission savings of a shared, consolidated location--inventory network under horizontal cooperation,
with \citet{hacardiaux2022balancing} balancing partner preferences over logistics cost and carbon footprint
in the same cooperative setting.
These studies make density or facility configuration the central decision and bring emissions into the
objective, the constraints, or an ex post assessment; where transport is modeled as an operational choice, it
enters through travel distances, discrete modes, or lead-time parameters rather than through a continuously
optimized replenishment speed chosen jointly with density.
The present study treats network density and replenishment speed as joint decisions and resolves the proximity
substitution between facilities and last-mile travel analytically.

\subsection{Inventory, transport mode, and emissions}

A third stream couples transport-mode and lead-time choices with inventory control under emissions regulation,
characterizing how carbon reshapes the trade-off between freight and the inventory it serves.
\citet{hoen2014effect} and \citet{chen2016effects} study transport mode selection alongside
inventory under stochastic demand and emissions regulation; \citet{bektas2011pollution} formalize the
load--emission relationship in the pollution-routing problem, embedding fuel use and emissions as a function
of vehicle load in the routing objective; \citet{moradi2025load} optimize driving speed and path selection
under load- and grade-dependent fuel use at the routing level; \citet{konur2014integrated} solve the economic
order quantity model with less-than-truckload and truckload carriers under carbon cap, tax, and cap-and-trade
regulation, and \citet{schaefer2015economic} extend this to a continuous-review, reorder-point inventory system under stochastic
demand, characterizing the cost--emission Pareto frontier and analyzing lead-time and demand-variance
sensitivity. \citet{drent2023efficient} analyze dynamic dual supply-mode selection under an emission target,
and \citet{hammami2015carbon} bring carbon from manufacturing, ordering, and inventory into a multi-echelon
production--inventory model with lead-time constraints. Across these papers, transport service is represented
through route/load physics, carrier class, discrete supply mode, or lead-time parameters and constraints; replenishment
speed is not a continuous decision chosen jointly with network density. The present study adds to this stream the continuous speed--density
threshold that results when replenishment speed and network density are chosen jointly under a carbon cap
and a service requirement.

\citet{boone2025freight} choose a discrete freight mode against inventory under an emission cap at a single
location, with no network-density decision. The present study replaces the finite mode set with a continuous
replenishment-speed decision, prices carbon on the holding as well as the transport side, embeds it in a network
whose density is chosen jointly, and characterizes the resulting continuous speed--density threshold and
complementarity.

\subsection{Carbon policy, service, and safety-stock placement}

A fourth stream prices carbon directly into inventory and service decisions, showing how a carbon price or cap
shifts order quantities and safety-stock placement.
\citet{hua2011managing} and \citet{bouchery2012including} develop carbon-aware order-quantity models under
cap-and-trade and multi-objective formulations, and \citet{benjaafar2013carbon} position cap, tax, and
cap-and-trade as priced operational instruments in simple supply-chain models; \citet{chen2013carbon} derive the
closed-form carbon-constrained economic order quantity, the single-location analog of the marginal
carbon-intensity logic we extend to a network, and \citet{arikan2014single} impose a product carbon-footprint
constraint on a dual-sourcing newsvendor. \citet{silbermayr2017inventory} bring environmental constraints into
inventory pooling with dependent demand. Within multi-echelon service models,
\citet{ni2015tradeoff} study the trade-off between service time and carbon in safety-stock placement under the
guaranteed-service framework, and \citet{wang2025inventory} incorporate cap-and-trade into guaranteed-service
placement. Probabilistic carbon limits have been modeled through chance constraints in lot sizing
\citep{koca2021stochastic} and in supply-chain network design \citep{shaw2016low}, typically inside large
mixed-integer programs; we instead obtain a closed-form deterministic equivalent that scales the transport component by a single factor. A recent review \citep{sebatjane2025sustainable} surveys carbon-aware inventory
models and the regulatory instruments they treat; across this body of work, transport-emission modeling, service and inventory, network design, and demand or
carbon uncertainty have developed in largely separate streams, and we bring these strands together. The service-placement models in particular price carbon against service
and safety stock, with the joint design of network density and replenishment speed outside their scope. 

Prior work covers carbon-aware density, mode and lead-time choice, service-constrained placement, and chance
constraints on carbon. The present study couples endogenous replenishment speed with network density under
carbon and service constraints and derives marginal carbon-intensity thresholds for the joint design. The
resulting diagnostic identifies which activity supplies availability and proximity when carbon is priced: a
speed threshold comparing the carbon intensity per dollar of holding with that of inbound transport, a
pooling-adjusted density threshold comparing last-mile intensity with a weighted average of facility and
holding intensities, and the complementarity that links them.
\section{The Model}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Operational environment}

\paragraph{Region and markets.} A firm serves $N$ markets distributed over a service region of area $A$.
Per-period demand at market $i$ is a random variable with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$,
identically distributed across markets with common pairwise correlation $\rho \in [0,1)$. Demand is
stationary and the setting is single-product. Markets and distribution centers are treated symmetrically, the standard
simplification that admits closed-form continuous-approximation results \citep{daganzo2005logistics}.


\paragraph{Distribution centers and geography.} The firm opens $n$ identical distribution centers (DCs), $1 \le n \le N$, each
serving a sub-region of area $A/n$. By the continuous-approximation spatial law, the mean last-mile
(DC-to-market) distance falls as the square root of the served area,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dn}
  d(n) \;=\; \delta\, n^{-1/2}, \qquad \delta \propto \sqrt{A},
\end{equation}
so a denser network shortens delivery but only at a decreasing rate \citep{daganzo2005logistics}.

\paragraph{Replenishment and the speed decision.} Each DC is replenished directly from the source at a chosen
speed $v \in [v_{\min}, v_{\max}]$. Individual DCs sit at different distances from the source; what enters
cost and carbon is total inbound freight: with identical DCs each carrying throughput $N\mu/n$, this is
$\sum_j (N\mu/n)\,D_j = N\mu\,\bar D$, the total throughput times the \emph{mean} source-to-DC distance
$\bar D$. Because the DCs fill a region of fixed
size served from a fixed source, $\bar D$ is set by the region's geometry (order $\sqrt A$) and is
approximately invariant to $n$; we write $D \equiv \bar D$ and treat it as constant.\footnote{Two
modeling reductions are embedded here. First, the symmetric-DC reduction assigns every DC the representative lead
time $L = \bar D/v$ in place of its own $L_j = D_j/v$. Safety stock enters through $\sqrt{L}$, which is
concave in distance, so by Jensen's inequality $\sqrt{\bar D} \ge n^{-1}\sum_j \sqrt{D_j}$
\citep{jensen1906fonctions}: using the mean distance slightly \emph{overstates} aggregate safety stock, by a
gap that is second-order in the dispersion of the $D_j$ and reduces as the distances equalize. Second, replenishment is
point-to-point; were the DCs served on a consolidated route, the inbound distance would grow as $\sqrt{n}$ in
the manner of the store-delivery tour of \citet{cachon2014retail}, adding a term that reinforces centralization
in Proposition~\ref{prop:density} and leaving the fixed-density speed comparison of
Proposition~\ref{prop:speed} unchanged; with inbound distance varying in $n$, however, the speed response
would depend on density through $D(n)$, so the complementarity of Proposition~\ref{prop:complement} need not
carry over unchanged.}
The replenishment lead time is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lead}
  L(v) \;=\; D/v .
\end{equation}
Faster replenishment is simultaneously more expensive and more carbon-intensive. The maintained assumption
is only that the inbound cost rate $t(v)$ and emission rate $\varepsilon(v)$ (both per unit per mile) are
increasing and convex in $v$; for the closed-form results, we use the quadratic instantiation
$\varepsilon(v)=\varepsilon_0 v^2$ and $t(v)=t_0+t_1 v^2$, the canonical form motivated by the cubic
fuel-speed law of slow steaming \citep{psaraftis2013speed, cariou2011slow}. We read $v$ as a general index
of replenishment speed --- vessel speed, or equivalently a faster and more carbon-intensive mode --- so
slow steaming is a motivating special case, not the only interpretation. Treating replenishment speed as a
continuously optimized decision, chosen jointly with network density, couples the transport and facility-and-holding components in this distribution setting.

\paragraph{Inventory and service.} Each DC operates a base-stock policy and must meet a cycle service level
$\alpha$ --- the in-stock probability at the end of a period, the Type-1 (cycle) service measure. With normally distributed demand the safety factor is $z = \Phi^{-1}(\alpha)$, with $\alpha>\tfrac12$ so
that $z>0$, taken here as a service requirement (a parameter). A DC that pools $g = N/n$ markets faces aggregate per-period demand with
standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{g\,[\,1+(g-1)\rho\,]}$, the statistical economy of scale in inventory
\citep{eppen1979effects}. Covering lead-time demand, each DC holds safety stock
$z\,\sigma\sqrt{L}\,\sqrt{g[1+(g-1)\rho]}$. Summing over the $n$ DCs and writing the \emph{pooling
expression}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi}
  \Psi(n) \;=\; N\rho + n(1-\rho),
\end{equation}
the system-wide safety stock is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ss}
  \sstock(n,v) \;=\; z\,\sigma\,\sqrt{D/v}\;\sqrt{N\,\Psi(n)} .
\end{equation}
At $\rho = 0$, \eqref{eq:ss} reduces to $z\sigma\sqrt{L}\sqrt{nN}$, the square-root law: decentralizing
from $1$ to $N$ DCs multiplies safety stock by $\sqrt{N}$. As $\rho \to 1$, $\Psi(n) \to N$ and safety stock
becomes invariant to $n$ --- the pooling benefit vanishes.

\paragraph{Decisions.} The firm chooses the network density $n \in [1,N]$ (relaxed to a continuous
variable and rounded) and the replenishment speed $v \in [v_{\min}, v_{\max}]$. Service is a requirement,
so $\alpha$ (hence $z$) is a parameter in our model. Table~\ref{tab:notation} collects the notation.

\subsection{Cost and carbon}

Both the cost and carbon functions are system-wide flows over a single
representative period, consistent with the stationary single-product environment above:
$F,\varphi$ are charged per DC per period, $h,\eta$ per unit held per period, and the transport terms accrue
against the per-period throughput. The representative distances in Figure~\ref{fig:setting} therefore enter
global cost and carbon through aggregate freight tasks, not through a per-territory objective. Throughput
itself is conserved at $N\mu$ regardless of the configuration: the firm moves the mean demand of all $N$
markets through the network, whatever density $n$ and speed $v$ it chooses. With per-period throughput
$N\mu$,
\begin{align}
  K(n,v) &= \underbrace{F\,n}_{\text{fixed}} + \underbrace{h\,\sstock(n,v)}_{\text{holding}}
            + \underbrace{t(v)\,N\mu D}_{\text{inbound}} + \underbrace{t_L\,N\mu\,d(n)}_{\text{last-mile}},
            \label{eq:K}\\[2pt]
  C(n,v) &= \underbrace{\varphi\,n}_{\text{fixed}} + \underbrace{\eta\,\sstock(n,v)}_{\text{holding}}
            + \underbrace{\varepsilon(v)\,N\mu D}_{\text{inbound}} + \underbrace{\varepsilon_L\,N\mu\,d(n)}_{\text{last-mile}},
            \label{eq:C}
\end{align}
where $F,\varphi$ are fixed cost/carbon per DC, $h,\eta$ are holding cost/carbon per unit per period, and
$t_L,\varepsilon_L$ are last-mile cost/carbon rates \citep{shen2003joint, hua2011managing,
benjaafar2013carbon}. Each term multiplies a rate by the activity it prices; the two functions differ only in
whether the rate is measured in dollars or kilograms.

\paragraph{Facilities.} The facility terms $F n$ and $\varphi n$ are the periodic cost and carbon of operating
the open DCs. They rise linearly in $n$ and do not depend on $v$. Facility carbon $\varphi$ is distinct from
holding carbon $\eta$: $\varphi$ captures baseline building energy and embodied carbon, whereas $\eta$ prices
the incremental carbon of storing a unit (for example refrigeration or handling).

\paragraph{Holding.} The holding terms $h\sstock(n,v)$ and $\eta\sstock(n,v)$ value the system-wide safety
stock of \eqref{eq:ss}. They rise in $n$ through the loss of pooling in $\Psi(n)$ and fall in $v$ through the
lead-time factor $\sqrt{D/v}$. This is the channel through which replenishment speed couples transport and
inventory.

\paragraph{Inbound transport.} The inbound terms multiply the representative freight task $N\mu D$ by the
speed-dependent rates $t(v)$ and $\varepsilon(v)$. Since $D$ is treated as invariant to $n$, inbound transport
does not vary with network density; it rises in $v$ through the increasing convex rates
$t(v)$ and $\varepsilon(v)$, opposing the holding term that falls in $v$.

\paragraph{Last-mile transport.} The last-mile terms multiply throughput $N\mu$ by the mean DC-to-market
distance $d(n)=\delta n^{-1/2}$ and the rates $t_L,\varepsilon_L$. A denser network shortens this leg at the
continuous-approximation rate, so the last-mile term is decreasing and convex in $n$ and independent of $v$
under constant per-mile last-mile rates.

\paragraph{Decomposition and scope.} The carbon function splits into a transport component
$\varepsilon(v)N\mu D+\varepsilon_LN\mu d(n)$ and a facility-and-holding component $\varphi n+\eta\sstock(n,v)$;
the cost function has the analogous partition. Slowing replenishment reduces inbound transport carbon but
raises safety stock, while dispersion reduces last-mile distance but raises facility count and pooling loss.
Three scope points follow. Cycle stock under a fixed review period is approximately $N\mu/2$ system-wide, or
$(N\mu/n)/2$ per DC, and is invariant to both decisions. Pipeline inventory $N\mu D/v$ is omitted; when valued
at the same dollar and carbon holding rates as stored inventory, it changes the level of $v^*$ but not the
speed threshold (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}). Production and procurement are also excluded because throughput
is fixed at $N\mu$ and therefore constant in both decisions.

\subsection{The program}

The firm minimizes cost subject to a carbon cap $B$ and the service requirement,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Pcap}
  \min_{\,1\le n\le N,\ v_{\min}\le v\le v_{\max}}\ K(n,v)
  \quad\text{s.t.}\quad C(n,v)\le B .
\end{equation}
Here $K$ and $C$ are expected flows over the model's accounting period: under the stationary demand assumption they are the long-run average cost and carbon of a configuration, and they can be
scaled to an annual expected-carbon cap by multiplying by the number of periods. Because demand is random,
realized carbon over a specified accounting horizon fluctuates about its expectation; we develop the analysis
for the expected-value cap and return below to a chance-constrained cap that bounds realized carbon with
specified confidence (Proposition~\ref{prop:chance}).

Equivalently, pricing carbon at $\tau \ge 0$ gives the priced objective $J=K+\tau C$. At an interior optimum,
the cap multiplier equals the price ($\lambda=\tau$), as in carbon-constrained inventory models
\citep{chen2013carbon}; sweeping $\tau$ traces the cost--carbon frontier within a stable interior regime,
with jumps possible at regime switches or integer-density changes. Define the blended rates
\[
  F_\tau=F+\tau\varphi,\qquad H_\tau=h+\tau\eta,\qquad
  T_\tau(v)=t(v)+\tau\varepsilon(v),\qquad
  \ell_\tau=t_L+\tau\varepsilon_L .
\]
Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:J}
  J(n,v;\tau) \;=\; F_\tau n + H_\tau\,\sstock(n,v)
     + T_\tau(v)N\mu D + \ell_\tau N\mu d(n).
\end{equation}
Setting $\tau=0$ recovers cost; the limit $\tau\to\infty$ recovers carbon up to scale.
\section{Analysis}\label{sec:analysis}

We now characterize how carbon pricing changes the joint design. The joint first-order conditions
decompose into two marginal substitutions: Proposition~\ref{prop:speed} gives the speed threshold on the
availability margin, Proposition~\ref{prop:service} records the service and volatility comparative static,
Proposition~\ref{prop:density} gives the pooling-adjusted density threshold on the proximity margin,
Proposition~\ref{prop:complement} establishes the complementarity through which the two margins interact,
and Corollary~\ref{cor:corner} characterizes the joint configuration when both comparisons agree. The
results are analytical under the stated interiority and convexity conditions, each threshold derived on its
margin with the other decision held at an arbitrary level; Section~\ref{sec:numerical} evaluates the joint
problem in calibrated numerical illustrations.

\begin{lemma}[Carbon intensity per dollar]\label{lem:blend}
Pricing carbon at $\tau$ replaces each cost rate in \eqref{eq:K} by the blended rate
$\mathrm{rate}_\$ + \tau\,\mathrm{rate}_{\mathrm{kg}}$, yielding the objective $J$ of \eqref{eq:J}. In the
interior comparisons below, each holding the other decision fixed, the \emph{direction} in which the carbon-minimizing choice departs
from the cost-minimizing one is governed by four carbon-intensity-per-dollar ratios --- holding $\eta/h$,
inbound transport $\varepsilon_0/t_1$, last-mile $\varepsilon_L/t_L$, and facilities $\varphi/F$ --- while the
magnitude and the realized joint optimum also depend on pooling, the bounds, and the complementarity of the
two decisions.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The blended rates in \eqref{eq:J} follow by collecting coefficients in $K+\tau C$. The speed comparison sets
holding against inbound transport at the margin (Proposition~\ref{prop:speed}); the density comparison sets
last-mile transport against a pooling-adjusted blend of facilities and holding
(Proposition~\ref{prop:density}). The four ratios listed in the statement are therefore the primitive
carbon-intensity comparisons used below.
\end{proof}

\noindent The carbon-minimizing choice on each decision therefore depends on each activity's carbon intensity
\emph{relative to its cost}, not on its absolute rates.

Writing $G(n,v)=\partial\sstock(n,v)/\partial n$, differentiation gives the stationarity conditions
\begin{align}
  F_\tau + H_\tau G(n,v) &= \frac{\ell_\tau N\mu\delta}{2}\,n^{-3/2}, \label{eq:focn}\\[2pt]
  N\mu D\,T_\tau'(v) &= \frac{H_\tau\,\sstock(n,v)}{2v}, \label{eq:focv}
\end{align}
the first balancing the marginal facility and lost-pooling costs against the marginal last-mile saving, and
the second balancing the marginal transport cost of speed against the marginal holding saving it obtains.
The joint first-order conditions thus reveal two marginal substitutions. The speed condition
\eqref{eq:focv} governs the \emph{availability margin}: safety stock and replenishment speed are
alternative ways to provide service. The density condition \eqref{eq:focn} governs the \emph{proximity
margin}: facilities and last-mile distance are alternative ways to reach markets. The thresholds below
compare the carbon intensity per dollar of the activities on each margin, and complementarity determines how
the two margins interact in the joint configuration.

\paragraph{Availability: inventory or speed.} The speed condition isolates the availability margin.
Increasing speed raises inbound transport cost and carbon but lowers the safety stock required for service;
the speed threshold identifies which activity is more carbon-intensive per dollar on this margin.

\begin{proposition}[Speed reversal]\label{prop:speed}
Fix the density $n$ and write the speed-dependent cost and carbon as
\[
  \hat K(v)=h\,\kappa(n)\,v^{-1/2}+N\mu D\,t(v), \qquad \hat C(v)=\eta\,\kappa(n)\,v^{-1/2}+N\mu D\,\varepsilon(v),
\]
with $\kappa(n)=z\sigma\sqrt{ND}\,\sqrt{\Psi(n)}$. If $t$ and $\varepsilon$ are increasing,
differentiable, and convex, then at a common density and for
interior optima the carbon-minimizing speed exceeds the cost-minimizing speed if and only if holding is more
carbon-intensive per dollar than inbound transport \emph{at the margin},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:speedthreshold}
  \frac{\eta}{h} \;>\; \frac{\varepsilon'(v_{\mathrm K})}{t'(v_{\mathrm K})},
\end{equation}
evaluated at the cost-minimizing speed $v_{\mathrm K}$. With the quadratic instantiation
$\varepsilon(v)=\varepsilon_0 v^2$, $t(v)=t_0+t_1 v^2$, the threshold reduces to $\eta/h>\varepsilon_0/t_1$ and
the unconstrained optimum is closed form,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vstar}
  v^*(n) \;=\; \left[\frac{(h+\tau\eta)\,z\sigma\sqrt{ND}\,\sqrt{\Psi(n)}}
                       {4\,(t_1+\tau\varepsilon_0)\,N\mu D}\right]^{2/5}.
\end{equation}
On $[v_{\min},v_{\max}]$ the optima are the projections of the unconstrained speeds onto the interval; under
the threshold \eqref{eq:speedthreshold} the comparison $v_C\ge v_K$ then holds weakly, and strictly when
neither projection binds.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$N\mu D\,t(v)$ and $N\mu D\,\varepsilon(v)$ are convex and $\kappa(n)v^{-1/2}$ has $\partial_v^2=\tfrac34 \kappa(n)v^{-5/2}>0$, so
both objectives are strictly convex in $v$. The cost optimum satisfies $\hat K'(v_{\mathrm K})=0$, i.e.\
$N\mu D\,t'(v_{\mathrm K})=\tfrac12 h\,\kappa(n)\,v_{\mathrm K}^{-3/2}$, so that $\tfrac12 \kappa(n)v_{\mathrm K}^{-3/2}=
N\mu D\,t'(v_{\mathrm K})/h$. Evaluating the carbon derivative there,
\[
  \hat C'(v_{\mathrm K})=-\eta\,\tfrac12 \kappa(n)v_{\mathrm K}^{-3/2}+N\mu D\,\varepsilon'(v_{\mathrm K})
  =N\mu D\big(\varepsilon'(v_{\mathrm K})-(\eta/h)\,t'(v_{\mathrm K})\big),
\]
which is negative --- so $v_C>v_{\mathrm K}$ by strict convexity --- if and only if \eqref{eq:speedthreshold}.
With $t'=2t_1 v$ and $\varepsilon'=2\varepsilon_0 v$ the marginal ratio is $\varepsilon_0/t_1$, and the priced
first-order condition \eqref{eq:focv} gives
$v^{5/2}=(h+\tau\eta)z\sigma\sqrt{ND}\,\sqrt{\Psi}/[4(t_1+\tau\varepsilon_0)N\mu D]$, which is
\eqref{eq:vstar}. The projection and the boundary qualification are immediate.
\end{proof}

\noindent Because \eqref{eq:vstar} depends on the carbon price only through the blended ratio
$(h+\tau\eta)/(t_1+\tau\varepsilon_0)$, which is monotone in $\tau$, the fixed-density speed optimum moves
monotonically between the cost and carbon endpoints as the carbon price rises: the endpoint comparison of the
proposition also orders every finite price.

\noindent Total carbon is U-shaped in speed. For products whose inventory is emissions-intensive ---
refrigerated, volatile, or held to a high service level --- a tighter carbon target can select \emph{faster}
replenishment. The comparison is at common density; in the joint problem, the density channel reinforces the
speed reversal when carbon also disperses the network and attenuates it otherwise
(Proposition~\ref{prop:complement}).

Service and volatility enter through the same channel. Write
$J^{\mathrm r}(n;s)\equiv J\big(n,v^*(n;s);s\big)$, with $s=z\sigma$, for the reduced objective obtained after
substituting the speed response into the priced objective.

\begin{proposition}[Service and volatility as carbon parameters]\label{prop:service}
Under the quadratic instantiation and for an interior speed response $v^*(n)\in(v_{\min},v_{\max})$,
$v^*(n)$ is increasing in the safety factor $z=\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)$ and in the demand standard deviation
$\sigma$, and both raise the holding term in \eqref{eq:focn}. At an interior reduced optimum (reduced
second-order condition $J^{\mathrm r}_{nn}>0$), this is a local
comparative-static result: a higher $z$ or $\sigma$ raises the inventory penalty of a denser network and shifts the carbon-minimizing density toward consolidation.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $s=z\sigma$. From \eqref{eq:vstar}, $v^*(n;s)\propto s^{2/5}$, so speed rises with service and demand
volatility. Along this speed response, $J^{\mathrm r}_n(n;s)=J_n\big(n,v^*(n;s);s\big)$ because $J_v=0$. The
holding marginal term in \eqref{eq:focn} is proportional to
$s(v^*)^{-1/2}(1-\rho)/\sqrt{\Psi(n)}\propto s^{4/5}(1-\rho)/\sqrt{\Psi(n)}$, hence rises in $s$ even after
the induced speed increase. Thus $J^{\mathrm r}_{ns}>0$. Under the reduced second-order condition
$J^{\mathrm r}_{nn}>0$, the implicit-function theorem gives
$\mathrm{d}n^*/\mathrm{d}s=-J^{\mathrm r}_{ns}/J^{\mathrm r}_{nn}<0$.
\end{proof}

\noindent A service requirement therefore affects the carbon-minimizing design as well as inventory cost;
endogenous service choice is a natural extension (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Proximity: facilities or last-mile travel.} The density condition isolates the proximity
margin. Increasing density shortens the last mile but raises facility cost and carbon and weakens pooling;
the density threshold identifies when carbon places more weight on last-mile distance than on the
facility-and-pooling cost of proximity, and pooling keeps the comparison from reducing to a single pair of
rates.

\begin{proposition}[Density and dispersion]\label{prop:density}
Fix the speed $v$ and let
\begin{equation}
  G(n,v)\;=\;\frac{\partial\,\sstock(n,v)}{\partial n}
           \;=\;z\sigma\sqrt{D/v}\,\sqrt{N}\,\frac{1-\rho}{2\sqrt{\Psi(n)}}\;\ge\;0
\end{equation}
be the marginal safety-stock penalty of an additional DC. The priced objective $J(\cdot,v)$ is strictly
convex in $n$ when
\begin{equation}\label{eq:densitysoc}
  3\ell_\tau N\mu\delta\,n^{-5/2}
  \;>\; H_\tau z\sigma\sqrt{D/v}\sqrt{N}\,(1-\rho)^2\,\Psi(n)^{-3/2}
  \qquad\text{for all }n\in[1,N],
\end{equation}
in which case the stationary point is unique; the minimizer is interior if and only if, in addition,
$J_n(1)<0<J_n(N)$, and otherwise lies at a boundary, $n^*\in\{1,N\}$. When \eqref{eq:densitysoc} holds at
$\tau=0$ and in the limit $\tau\to\infty$, and for interior cost and carbon
density optima, the carbon-minimizing density exceeds the cost-minimizing density, $n_C(v)>n_K(v)$, if and
only if last-mile transport is more carbon-intensive per dollar than the \emph{pooling-adjusted facility threshold},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:densthreshpool}
  \frac{\varepsilon_L}{t_L} \;>\; \frac{\varphi+\eta\,G\big(n_K(v),v\big)}{F+h\,G\big(n_K(v),v\big)} .
\end{equation}
The right-hand side is a weighted average of the facility ratio $\varphi/F$ and the holding ratio $\eta/h$,
with weight on $\eta/h$ rising in the pooling penalty $G$: it equals $\varphi/F$ when pooling is absent and
tilts monotonically toward $\eta/h$ as $G$ grows (lower correlation, higher safety factor or volatility, or
slower replenishment). In the no-pooling limit ($\rho\to1$, so $G\to0$) the optimum is closed form,
$n^*=(\ell_\tau N\mu\delta/2F_\tau)^{2/3}$ (with the blended last-mile and facility rates
$\ell_\tau=t_L+\tau\varepsilon_L$ and $F_\tau=F+\tau\varphi$ of \eqref{eq:J}), and
\eqref{eq:densthreshpool} reduces to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:densitythreshold}
  \frac{\varepsilon_L}{t_L} \;>\; \frac{\varphi}{F}.
\end{equation}
The ordering $n_C(v)>n_K(v)$ is strict at interior optima; when an optimum lies at a boundary ($n=1$ or $N$)
it holds as a projected weak ordering.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since
\[
  J_{nn}=\frac34\,\ell_\tau N\mu\delta\,n^{-5/2}
  -\frac14\,H_\tau z\sigma\sqrt{D/v}\sqrt{N}\,(1-\rho)^2\Psi(n)^{-3/2},
\]
\eqref{eq:densitysoc} gives strict convexity and the stated interior/boundary characterization. Because
$(t_L+\tau\varepsilon_L)/(h+\tau\eta)$ is monotone in $\tau$, the condition at the two endpoints implies it
for every $\tau$. For the
threshold, take $\tau=0$ for cost and $\tau\to\infty$ for carbon. The cost density satisfies $\partial_n K=F+h\,G(n_K,v)-
t_L N\mu\delta/(2n_K^{3/2})=0$, so the last-mile marginal $t_L N\mu\delta/(2n_K^{3/2})=F+h\,G(n_K,v)$.
Evaluating the carbon derivative there,
\[
  \partial_n C\big|_{n_K}
  =\varphi+\eta\,G(n_K,v)-\frac{\varepsilon_L N\mu\delta}{2n_K^{3/2}}
  =\varphi+\eta\,G(n_K,v)-\frac{\varepsilon_L}{t_L}\,\big[F+h\,G(n_K,v)\big],
\]
which is negative --- so $n_C>n_K$ by convexity --- if and only if \eqref{eq:densthreshpool}. Writing
$R(G)=(\varphi+\eta G)/(F+hG)$, we have $R(0)=\varphi/F$, $R(G)\to\eta/h$ as $G\to\infty$, and
$R'(G)=(\eta F-h\varphi)/(F+hG)^2$, so $R$ is monotone with $\operatorname{sign}R'=\operatorname{sign}
(\eta/h-\varphi/F)$ and lies between $\varphi/F$ and $\eta/h$. In the no-pooling limit $\rho\to1$, $G\to0$ and $\Psi\to N$, so
\eqref{eq:focn} gives $n^*=(\ell_\tau N\mu\delta/2F_\tau)^{2/3}$ and \eqref{eq:densthreshpool} becomes \eqref{eq:densitythreshold}.
\end{proof}

\noindent The density decision is the centralize-versus-decentralize question in carbon terms. By
\eqref{eq:densthreshpool}, the carbon-minimizing network is more dispersed when the last-mile ratio
$\varepsilon_L/t_L$ exceeds a facility threshold that pooling shifts from $\varphi/F$ toward $\eta/h$.
Pooling therefore enters as an explicit weight on the holding ratio, not as a uniform centralizing force: when
holding is more carbon-intensive per dollar than facilities, stronger pooling raises the threshold for
dispersion; otherwise it lowers it. A corner optimum, when it arises, is fully centralized or fully
decentralized.

\paragraph{Complementarity of speed and density.} The two margins interact through safety stock: faster
replenishment reduces the inventory penalty of density, and higher density increases the inventory exposure
affected by speed. Proposition~\ref{prop:complement} makes the interaction precise.

\begin{proposition}[Complementarity]\label{prop:complement}
$v^*(n)$ is increasing in $n$, and under the density second-order condition $n^*(v)$ is increasing in $v$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $G(n,v)\propto v^{-1/2}$, the cross-partial is $J_{nv}=H_\tau G_v=-H_\tau G(n,v)/(2v)<0$ for
$\rho<1$. For the speed response, $J$ is strictly convex in $v$ (as in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:speed}), so by the implicit function theorem
$\mathrm{d}v^*/\mathrm{d}n=-J_{nv}/J_{vv}>0$. For the density response, under the second-order condition
$J_{nn}>0$ of \eqref{eq:densitysoc}, $\mathrm{d}n^*/\mathrm{d}v=-J_{nv}/J_{nn}>0$.
\end{proof}

\noindent Faster replenishment shrinks safety stock and lowers the inventory penalty of dispersion; a denser
network raises the inventory at stake and raises the value of speed. Speed investments and network density are
therefore best evaluated jointly.

\paragraph{The joint regime.} The two marginal comparisons can point in any combination of directions.
The corollary below records a sufficient condition under which the carbon best-response equilibrium lies
weakly above the cost optimum in both network density and replenishment speed.

\begin{corollary}[Faster and more dispersed]\label{cor:corner}
Suppose, over the relevant range of configurations, the speed threshold of Proposition~\ref{prop:speed} holds
at every density and the pooling-adjusted density threshold \eqref{eq:densthreshpool} of
Proposition~\ref{prop:density} holds at every speed, so that the carbon-minimizing best responses dominate the
cost-minimizing ones pointwise, $v_C(n)\ge v_K(n)$ for all $n$ and $n_C(v)\ge n_K(v)$ for all $v$. Suppose
also that the density second-order condition \eqref{eq:densitysoc} holds at the carbon rates and that the
carbon best responses are single-valued and continuous on the interval reached by the iteration. Then best-response iteration from the cost optimum converges to a carbon best-response
equilibrium that is weakly faster and weakly more dispersed than the cost-minimizing configuration, strictly
so at interior optima.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The two thresholds give the pointwise dominance $v_C(n)\ge v_K(n)$ and $n_C(v)\ge n_K(v)$
(Propositions~\ref{prop:speed} and~\ref{prop:density}), and by Proposition~\ref{prop:complement} each best
response is nondecreasing ($v^*(\cdot)$ in $n$, $n^*(\cdot)$ in $v$). Starting from the cost optimum
$(n_K,v_K)$, a fixed point of the cost best responses, and iterating the carbon best responses gives a
nondecreasing sequence: $v_C(n_K)\ge v_K(n_K)=v_K$, then $n_C\!\big(v_C(n_K)\big)\ge n_C(v_K)\ge n_K(v_K)=n_K$,
and so on. The iterates are monotone and bounded, hence converge; by continuity of the single-valued
best-response maps, the limit is a fixed point of the carbon best-response map and is weakly no smaller than
$(n_K,v_K)$
\citep{topkis1998}, with strict inequalities at interior optima.
\end{proof}

\noindent The corollary is a best-response result under the stated conditions. A global optimum statement
would require convexity over the whole feasible box $[1,N]\times[v_{\min},v_{\max}]$; the speed--pooling
cross term $J_{nv}=H_\tau G_v$ can exceed the own curvatures in the extreme high-density, low-speed corner.

\noindent In the calibrated illustrations, the finite-grid computation finds the stated configuration as the
lowest sampled priced objective and verifies positive Hessian minors at the sampled points around the reported
optimum. These checks document the sampled configurations used in Section~\ref{sec:numerical} rather than
extend the analytical corollary.

\noindent A refrigerated, volatile, high-service product delivered by diesel van illustrates this regime: its
carbon-minimizing chain replenishes faster and holds stock closer to demand. The contribution is the
diagnostic structure, not the universality of this regime (Section~\ref{sec:managerial}).

\paragraph{Demand uncertainty: a chance-constrained cap.} The cost and carbon functions are expected
per-period flows, so the cap in \eqref{eq:Pcap} bounds \emph{expected} carbon. Because demand is random,
realized carbon fluctuates about $C(n,v)$: the facility charge and the base-stock safety-stock target are
planned and treated as deterministic, but inbound and last-mile freight scale with realized throughput
$Q=\sum_i X_i$, the sum of the realized market demands, which has mean $N\mu$ and variance
$\sigma_Q^2=\sigma^2 N\,[\,1+(N-1)\rho\,]$. Realized carbon is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Ctilde}
  \tilde C(n,v) \;=\; \varphi n + \eta\,\sstock(n,v) + \big[\varepsilon(v)D+\varepsilon_L d(n)\big]\,Q,
\end{equation}
and a firm that requires realized carbon to stay within $B$ with confidence $1-\beta_c$ --- where $\beta_c$ is
the carbon-side tail probability, written with the subscript $c$ to distinguish it from the inventory
fill-rate $\beta$ of the lot-sizing literature --- imposes the chance
constraint $\Pr\{\tilde C(n,v)\le B\}\ge 1-\beta_c$, the carbon-side counterpart of the cycle service level
$\alpha$ on the inventory side.

\begin{proposition}[Deterministic equivalent of the chance constraint]\label{prop:chance}
This chance constraint is equivalent to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chancecap}
  \varphi n + \eta\,\sstock(n,v) + m\big[\varepsilon(v)N\mu D+\varepsilon_L N\mu\, d(n)\big]\;\le\;B,
\end{equation}
the carbon function \eqref{eq:C} with the transport component scaled by $m=q_{1-\beta_c}/(N\mu)$, where
$q_{1-\beta_c}$ is the $(1-\beta_c)$ quantile of realized throughput $Q$, and the facility and holding terms
unchanged. Under the normal approximation $Q\sim\mathcal N(N\mu,\sigma_Q^2)$ this is
$m=1+z_{\beta_c}\,\mathrm{CV}_Q$, with $z_{\beta_c}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\beta_c)$ and $\mathrm{CV}_Q=\sigma_Q/(N\mu)$; then
$m\ge 1$ whenever $\beta_c\le\tfrac12$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In \eqref{eq:Ctilde} the throughput coefficient $\gamma(n,v)=\varepsilon(v)D+\varepsilon_L d(n)>0$, so
$\{\tilde C\le B\}=\{Q\le(B-\varphi n-\eta\sstock)/\gamma\}$, and $\Pr\{\tilde C\le B\}\ge 1-\beta_c$ holds iff
$(B-\varphi n-\eta\sstock)/\gamma\ge q_{1-\beta_c}$, i.e.\ $\varphi n+\eta\sstock+\gamma\,q_{1-\beta_c}\le B$. Writing
$\gamma\,q_{1-\beta_c}=(q_{1-\beta_c}/N\mu)\,[\varepsilon(v)N\mu D+\varepsilon_L N\mu d(n)]$ gives \eqref{eq:chancecap}.
Under normality $q_{1-\beta_c}=N\mu+z_{\beta_c}\sigma_Q$, so $m=1+z_{\beta_c}\,\mathrm{CV}_Q$.
\end{proof}

\noindent The chance constraint therefore leaves the structure intact: \eqref{eq:chancecap} is the
four-component carbon function with the inbound and last-mile carbon rates scaled, $\varepsilon_0\mapsto
m\varepsilon_0$ and $\varepsilon_L\mapsto m\varepsilon_L$, while cost and the facility and holding terms are
unchanged. Because $Q$ is realized market throughput, exogenous to the firm's choice of $n$ and $v$, the
factor $m$ is a decision-invariant scalar on the transport carbon rates; the priced objective is again
\eqref{eq:J} under this substitution, so Propositions~\ref{prop:speed}--\ref{prop:complement} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:corner} carry over, and only the two thresholds shift.

\begin{corollary}[Uncertainty tightens the speed threshold and relaxes the density threshold]\label{cor:chance}
Under the chance-constrained cap the speed threshold \eqref{eq:speedthreshold} becomes
$\eta/h>m\,\varepsilon'(v_{\mathrm K})/t'(v_{\mathrm K})$ ($m\,\varepsilon_0/t_1$ under the quadratic), and the
pooling-adjusted density threshold \eqref{eq:densthreshpool} becomes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chancedensthresh}
  m\,\frac{\varepsilon_L}{t_L} \;>\; \frac{\varphi+\eta\,G\big(n_K(v),v\big)}{F+h\,G\big(n_K(v),v\big)},
\end{equation}
which reduces to $m\,\varepsilon_L/t_L>\varphi/F$ in the no-pooling limit ($\rho\to1$). Under the normal approximation, $m\ge 1$ for
$\beta_c\le\tfrac12$, with equality at $\beta_c=\tfrac12$, so the chance constraint tightens the speed-reversal
threshold and relaxes the dispersion threshold relative to the expected-value cap, and the effect grows as $m$ rises with the demand
coefficient of variation $\sigma/\mu$, the correlation $\rho$, and the confidence $1-\beta_c$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The chance constraint multiplies the inbound and last-mile carbon rates by $m$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:chance}); substituting $m\varepsilon_0$ and $m\varepsilon_L$ for $\varepsilon_0$ and
$\varepsilon_L$ in \eqref{eq:speedthreshold} and \eqref{eq:densthreshpool} gives the two conditions, the latter
scaling only the left side and leaving the pooling-adjusted facility threshold $(\varphi+\eta G)/(F+hG)$ on
the right. From $\sigma_Q^2=\sigma^2N[1+(N-1)\rho]$, $\mathrm{CV}_Q=(\sigma/\mu)\sqrt{[1+(N-1)\rho]/N}$ is
increasing in $\sigma/\mu$ and, for $N>1$, in $\rho$, and $z_{\beta_c}$ is increasing in $1-\beta_c$, so
$m=1+z_{\beta_c}\mathrm{CV}_Q$ is increasing in all three.
\end{proof}

\noindent Demand uncertainty therefore affects carbon through two channels. It enters the inventory-holding component through the safety stock
$z\sigma\sqrt{L}$, the channel behind the speed reversal, and, under a chance-constrained cap, it enters the
transport component through the margin $z_{\beta_c}\,\mathrm{CV}_Q$ that the firm holds against realized emissions
just as it holds safety stock against demand. More variable or more correlated demand raises this margin and,
like a higher service requirement, shifts the carbon-minimizing configuration; the expected-value cap is the
limiting case $m=1$. Carrying the realized variability of on-hand inventory as well would place an analogous
margin on the inventory-holding component; we keep the safety stock at its planned level, consistent with the base-stock
policy. The deterministic equivalent itself is distribution-free in the throughput quantile $q_{1-\beta_c}$;
the normal approximation enters only through the closed form $m=1+z_{\beta_c}\,\mathrm{CV}_Q$, which is most
accurate for moderate $N$ and modest $\mathrm{CV}_Q$ and which a firm with strongly skewed demand can replace
by the empirical throughput quantile without altering the structure.

The cap applies over the same accounting period as the flow model --- here a week. For a cap on cumulative
carbon over $T$ periods with demands independent across periods, aggregate throughput has coefficient of
variation $\mathrm{CV}_Q/\sqrt{T}$, so the multiplier tightens toward one as the horizon lengthens: at the
baseline calibration, the $95\%$ multiplier falls from $m=1.37$ for a weekly cap to $m=1.05$ for an annual
cap ($T=52$). The chance-constrained results are therefore most relevant to high-frequency operational
carbon budgets, shipment-cycle accounting, and corporate carbon controls, and less binding for annual
compliance accounting.

\paragraph{In-transit inventory.} The holding term is modeled through safety stock; in-transit (pipeline)
inventory, $N\mu L = N\mu D/v$, falls in $v$ and is omitted from our model. If pipeline inventory were carried at the warehouse
holding rates, it would add $(h+\tau\eta)\,N\mu D/v$ to the holding term; the speed first-order condition
then acquires a $v^{-2}$ term, and stationarity again equates $(h+\tau\eta)/(t_1+\tau\varepsilon_0)$ to a
quantity strictly increasing in $v$ --- the same ratio that governs \eqref{eq:speedthreshold}. Valued at
common rates, pipeline inventory therefore shifts the level of $v^*$ (the closed form \eqref{eq:vstar} is
replaced by a numerical root) but preserves the carbon-intensity threshold; carrying capital cost with
negligible warehouse carbon instead raises only the cost-minimizing speed, shifting the threshold against
the speed reversal. We treat pipeline inventory as a level effect under the equal-rate convention and leave
alternative carbon accounting for in-transit stock as an extension.

\paragraph{Load-factor sensitivity.} Our model treats the per-unit last-mile rates $t_L$ and $\varepsilon_L$
as independent of shipment size. Last-mile cost and carbon, however, accrue largely per \emph{vehicle}-mile
--- driver time and fuel --- so the per-unit rates scale with vehicle-miles per unit, which rise as
dispersion thins each territory's dispatch volume and average vehicle fill falls; load-sensitive rates therefore
introduce a consolidation force outside the constant-rate model. Because a fill multiplier depends on $n$,
it enters the marginal density condition through both the level and the derivative of the last-mile term.
When a common fill multiplier is applied to the cost and the carbon rate alike, both optima shift toward
consolidation in the reported computation, while their relative direction remains consistent with the
carbon-intensity comparison. Per vehicle-mile, cost is dominated by driver time while fuel use falls somewhat in an
emptier vehicle --- the basis of tonne-kilometre emission accounting \citep{glec2022framework} --- so when
fill deteriorates, the per-unit cost rate typically inflates at least as strongly as the per-unit carbon
rate. A multiplier falling more heavily on carbon is therefore a stress case for the dispersion result; under
it, the density comparison can shift toward consolidation. Section~\ref{sec:numerical} reports
a reduced-form sensitivity computation for both cases; a full treatment would make shipment size, routing,
and consolidation additional decisions, which we leave as an extension.
\section{Numerical Illustrations}\label{sec:numerical}

This section applies the analytical thresholds to calibrated products, reports the implied
designs and cost--carbon trade-offs, and shows how the mechanisms interact under the maintained model
assumptions. The dollar magnitudes are chosen so that the
cost-minimizing network is interior, and the four carbon-intensity-per-dollar ratios are set to
regime-representative values anchored in published logistics ranges --- line-haul and last-mile transport,
refrigerated and ambient storage, and facility energy. All reported quantities are computed from the cost and
carbon functions of Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:analysis} by a finite-grid computation over the two
decision variables $n$ and $v$.

\subsection{Calibration}

Table~\ref{tab:calib} lists the baseline parameters. Units are normalized: the period is a week, and the
inbound leg is the unit of distance ($D=1$), so $t_0$, $t_1$, and $\varepsilon_0$ are per-unit coefficients
over that leg rather than per mile, while the last-mile rates $t_L$ and $\varepsilon_L$ are per unit-mile
against the $d(n)=\delta n^{-1/2}$ miles of the delivery leg. The four ratios give $R_{\mathrm{speed}}=(\eta/h)/
(\varepsilon_0/t_1)=0.375$ and $R_{\mathrm{density}}=(\varepsilon_L/t_L)/(\varphi/F)=0.75$, placing the
baseline in the slower-and-more-consolidated regime, where both comparisons fall short of one. Solving \eqref{eq:Pcap}, the
cost-minimizing configuration is $n^*=7.8$ distribution centers replenished at speed $v^*=1.03$, with weekly
cost \$1.24M and carbon $160.5$~t, divided between a transport component of $85.6$~t and a facility-and-holding component of
$74.9$~t. The carbon-minimizing configuration is slower and more consolidated, $n^*=6.6$ and $v^*=0.69$,
lowering carbon by $5.1\%$ at a cost increase of $1.2\%$. The movement is modest because the two components
substitute: slowing or consolidating shifts carbon from one component to the other rather than removing it, so
for a product in this regime the net change is small. The magnitude of available abatement is
regime-dependent, as the archetypes below show. The strict-convexity condition \eqref{eq:densitysoc} compares
last-mile curvature with the holding pooling-loss term, and is favored by a geographically spread region
(large $\delta$) or lower demand variability (small $\sigma/\mu$); it is weakest when pooling is strong and the
region compact.\footnote{Explicitly, at a given speed $v$ the binding case of
\eqref{eq:densitysoc} is $n=N$, where it reduces to
$\delta>H_\tau z\sigma\sqrt{D/v}\sqrt{N}(1-\rho)^2/[3\ell_\tau\mu]$. At
the baseline this requires $\delta>82$ at the carbon optimum and $\delta>67$ at the cost optimum, both met by
$\delta=120$; only the slowest speeds at the highest density (the non-convex corner noted after
Corollary~\ref{cor:corner}) require a larger region.} It holds at every configuration reported in this section --- the baseline and all four archetypes --- so each density
optimum is the unique interior stationary point.

\subsection{Product archetypes}

The four archetypes are guided by external benchmarks: the refrigerated-warehouse energy premium places
cold-chain and e-grocery goods in the high-$\eta/h$ band; low-fill diesel vans raise $\varepsilon_L/t_L$ for
e-grocery, pharmaceutical, and bulky goods; value density enters through the capital component of $h$; and
the line-haul mode factors order inbound carbon against speed.\footnote{Refrigerated warehouses run at
roughly $3.7\times$ the ambient energy intensity ($\approx 8.6$ versus $2.3$ kg CO$_2$e/ft$^2$/yr)
\citep{energystar2024medians, epa2025egrid}. A delivery van emits $\approx 0.62$ kg CO$_2$e/t-km at
$37$--$41\%$ fill, roughly $6$--$8\times$ an articulated HGV
\citep{defra2024ghg, defra2024methodology, glec2022framework}. Value density spans crushed stone at
$\approx\$17.5$/tonne \citep{usgs2025stone} to pharmaceuticals at $\approx\$49{,}000$/tonne
\citep{faf2023framework}; because the capital cost of inventory is part of $h$, cold-chain pharma's high
$\eta/h$ assumes its refrigeration and compliance energy dominates the capital denominator. Line-haul
factors order ocean $0.016<$ rail $0.028<$ road $0.10$--$0.21$ kg CO$_2$e/t-km \citep{defra2025ghg};
$\varepsilon_0/t_1$ is not separately identified. Supporting value, fill, and cost benchmarks are from
\citet{worldbank2023roadfreight,bts2012cfs,cushman2026industrial}. The assumed ratios
$(\eta/h,\,\varepsilon_0/t_1,\,\varepsilon_L/t_L,\,\varphi/F)$ are $(0.30,0.80,0.25,0.40)$ for ambient dry
goods, $(0.95,0.50,1.40,0.35)$ for cold-chain pharma, $(0.20,0.45,1.30,0.30)$ for bulky low-value goods, and
$(0.85,0.55,1.50,0.30)$ for e-grocery perishables.} The emission factors are well established, while the
dollar denominators are normalized order-of-magnitude cost proxies, so the benchmarks set the
\emph{relative} magnitudes of the ratios across archetypes; the chosen values are regime-representative
calibrations that span the four regimes.

Table~\ref{tab:archetypes} reports the diagnostics and the carbon-minimizing outcomes. Because the
archetypes share the cost rates, all four have the same cost-minimizing configuration ($n^*=7.8$,
$v^*=1.03$) and differ only in the direction and magnitude by which the carbon-minimizing configuration
departs from it. Slowing and consolidating is carbon-minimizing for the ambient dry-goods archetype; the
cold-chain and e-grocery archetypes call for faster and more dispersed networks; and the bulky, low-value
archetype, with inexpensive storage but a carbon-intensive last mile, disperses while slowing. Available
abatement ranges from about $6\%$ to $17\%$ of total carbon across the four; reaching $80\%$ of that
abatement costs between $0.5\%$ and $2.2\%$, while reaching the carbon minimum costs between $1.5\%$ and
$7.8\%$, the larger premiums attaching to the products that disperse most. All four archetypes have interior
density optima ($J_n(1)<0<J_n(N)$), so none is a corner solution.

\subsection{Chance-constrained carbon caps}

Moving from an expected carbon cap to a cap on \emph{realized} carbon held with high confidence is not merely
a tightening of the same constraint; by Proposition~\ref{prop:chance} it scales the transport component by
$m=1+z_{\beta_c}\,\mathrm{CV}_Q$ and so reweights transport emissions relative to inventory and facilities,
changing the relative attractiveness of speed and density. We trace this for the baseline product. With
$N=40$, $\mu=20{,}000$, $\sigma=8{,}000$ (CV $0.40$), and $\rho=0.30$, the aggregate throughput coefficient
of variation is
\[
  \mathrm{CV}_Q=\frac{\sigma}{\mu}\sqrt{\frac{1+(N-1)\rho}{N}}\approx 0.225,
\]
so the transport scalar is $m\approx 1.29$, $1.37$, and $1.52$ at the $90\%$, $95\%$, and $99\%$ confidence
levels ($z_{\beta_c}\approx 1.28,\,1.64,\,2.33$), against $m=1$ for the expected cap.

Table~\ref{tab:chance} reports the carbon-minimizing design under the scaled carbon function at each
confidence level, with $\varepsilon_0$ and $\varepsilon_L$ replaced by $m\varepsilon_0$ and $m\varepsilon_L$.
Two effects appear, consistent with Corollary~\ref{cor:chance}. On speed, the effective inbound ratio
$m\varepsilon_0/t_1$ rises from $0.80$ to $1.22$, moving further above the holding ratio $\eta/h=0.30$, so the
carbon-minimizing speed falls at every confidence level, from $0.69$ to $0.59$. On density, the effective
last-mile ratio $m\varepsilon_L/t_L$ rises from $0.30$ to $0.46$, raising the risk-weighted last-mile term
against the pooling-adjusted facility threshold of Proposition~\ref{prop:density}. That proposition compares
density best responses at a \emph{fixed} speed; the table instead reports the \emph{joint} carbon-minimizing
density $n_C$ against the joint cost-minimizing density $n_K$, in which the speed also adjusts. By the
fixed-speed test, dispersion would set in already at the $90\%$ cap, where $m\varepsilon_L/t_L=0.39$ clears
the pooling-adjusted threshold ($\approx0.37$ at the cost optimum); on the joint basis the risk margin also
lowers the speed, and by complementarity (Proposition~\ref{prop:complement}) a slower chain calls for fewer
warehouses, which partly offsets the dispersion, so the joint $n_C$ rises through $n_K$ later, between the
$90\%$ and $95\%$ levels. For this product a high-confidence carbon cap therefore calls for a chain that is
slower and progressively less consolidated, and beyond a threshold confidence more dispersed than the cost
optimum --- a change in direction produced by the accounting rule alone, with the physical parameters held
fixed. Demand uncertainty thus enters the carbon design through two channels: safety stock on the inventory
side and a throughput-risk margin on the transport side. Figure~\ref{fig:chance} traces the two designs
across confidence levels.

\subsection{Robustness}

The robustness analysis comprises four checks. Restricting the network to an integer number of DCs returns
the continuous result rounded: cost $(8,\,1.04)$, carbon $(7,\,0.69)$. Restricting replenishment to three
discrete modes ($v\in\{0.5,1.0,2.0\}$) leaves the cost optimum at the standard mode, $(7.8,\,1.0)$, and
shifts the carbon optimum to the slow mode, $(6.3,\,0.5)$, the discrete counterpart of the continuous speed
reduction. As the demand correlation rises from $\rho=0$ to $\rho=0.9$, both optima add facilities as
pooling weakens --- the cost-minimizing density from $5.9$ to $9.8$ and the carbon-minimizing density from
$4.8$ to $8.1$ --- with the carbon optimum consolidated relative to the cost optimum throughout.
The fourth check is the load-factor
sensitivity of Section~\ref{sec:analysis}, computed with the reduced-form fill multiplier
$0.5+0.5\,(n/n_{\mathrm{ref}})$, $n_{\mathrm{ref}}=8$ (half load-sensitive at the reference density).
Applying the multiplier to the carbon rate only --- the stress case of Section~\ref{sec:analysis} --- lowers
the carbon-minimizing density sharply, from $6.6$ to
$3.0$ DCs for the baseline and from $22.8$ to $5.8$ for the bulky archetype, reversing the bulky archetype's
dispersion relative to the unadjusted cost optimum. Applying the same multiplier to the cost and carbon
rates alike consolidates the cost optimum as well, from $7.8$ to $3.4$ DCs, and in this computation each
product's direction is preserved: the baseline still consolidates and the bulky archetype still disperses
relative to the load-adjusted cost optimum. Load sensitivity thus shifts both optima toward consolidation;
in these illustrations, reversal occurs only in the stress case, when the fill multiplier falls more heavily
on carbon.

\paragraph{Using the diagnostic ratios.} A firm can use the ratios, estimated from its own rate data, as
diagnostics for the joint design problem. The holding-to-inbound comparison indicates the carbon pressure on
the availability margin (Proposition~\ref{prop:speed}). The last-mile-to-facility comparison, adjusted for
pooling, indicates the carbon pressure on the proximity margin: pooling shifts the threshold from
$\varphi/F$ toward the holding ratio $\eta/h$, raising the bar for dispersing when $\eta/h>\varphi/F$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:density}). Because speed changes the inventory cost of density, and density changes
the inventory value of speed, the two margins are evaluated together in the final configuration
(Proposition~\ref{prop:complement}). Under a cap on realized carbon, the transport intensities are first
scaled by the throughput-risk multiplier (Proposition~\ref{prop:chance}).

\paragraph{Replenishment speed.} For refrigerated, volatile, or high-service products, a tighter carbon
target can be met through faster shipping: the safety stock a slower mode would require carries more carbon
than the faster transport adds (the top row of Table~\ref{tab:regimes}).

\paragraph{Consolidation and the last mile.} Consolidation lowers carbon here
through inventory pooling and reduced facility energy while lengthening the last mile. A firm whose last
mile is carbon-intensive --- diesel vans, long rural routes --- can lower total carbon by holding more stock
nearer to demand, even though that raises total inventory; the transport channel of consolidation works
against it. This last channel is strong when vehicle fill improves with consolidation, and the
load-factor sensitivity of Section~\ref{sec:analysis} separates its two effects: a fill multiplier common to
cost and carbon consolidates both optima together, while a multiplier that falls more heavily on carbon --- a
stress case, since per-vehicle-mile cost is typically at least as fill-sensitive as fuel --- can
shift the density comparison toward consolidation --- in the carbon-only computation, the bulky archetype's
carbon-minimizing network falls from $22.8$ to $5.8$ DCs (Section~\ref{sec:numerical}). A firm should
therefore weigh its last mile's fill economics --- and whether fill degrades its emissions faster than its
cost --- before dispersing.

\paragraph{Joint adjustment of the two margins.} Because the margins are complements, a program that
acts on only one margin --- a slow-steaming mandate or a consolidation initiative --- attains part of the available
abatement, and for some products presses a margin against its carbon-minimizing direction: for the e-grocery
archetype, speed alone abates at most $0.5\%$ and density alone $8.4\%$, while the joint design reaches
$9.6\%$ and meets a common target at lower cost (Table~\ref{tab:joint}).

\paragraph{Product segmentation, service, and volatility.} Because the carbon-minimizing configuration
depends on the product, a single chain-wide policy serves a heterogeneous portfolio imperfectly. The
service-level target and demand variability also bear on carbon: a higher service-level target and a more
volatile demand both enlarge safety stock and so, by Proposition~\ref{prop:service}, raise the
carbon-minimizing speed and strengthen the pull toward consolidation through the value of pooling, unless the
last-mile comparison prevails in the density decision. A $99\%$ cycle-service target is thus a carbon-design
parameter, not only an inventory-cost one. Higher demand correlation across markets weakens the pooling
benefit that favors consolidation and can make dispersion more attractive, so forecasting and
demand-smoothing bear on carbon in their own right.

\paragraph{Carbon accounting and confidence.} Whether the cap binds expected or realized carbon is itself
a design parameter: a high-confidence cap places a risk margin on transport emissions, tightening the speed
threshold and relaxing the dispersion threshold (Corollary~\ref{cor:chance}), and for the baseline product
it shifts the network from consolidation to dispersion beyond a threshold confidence
(Table~\ref{tab:chance}).

\paragraph{Implication for policy.} Our results also suggest a caution for regulation. A rule that
prices or caps only transport leaves the facility-and-holding component untouched and can induce substitution into inventory
and facilities, potentially raising emissions the rule does not measure. Formally, a transport-only instrument
prices $C_{\mathrm T}(n,v)=\varepsilon(v)\,N\mu D+\varepsilon_L\,N\mu\,d(n)$; minimizing $K+\tau C_{\mathrm T}$
blends the carbon price into the transport rates alone, so the induced response can raise the unpriced holding
and facility carbon. In the baseline calibration a transport-only price lowers transport carbon while raising total carbon.
Instruments scoped to transport alone
--- a freight fuel levy, a mode-shift mandate, or a vehicle-emissions standard --- are exposed to this
substitution. An instrument that prices a firm's total logistics carbon (for example, a scope-inclusive
carbon charge or a cap covering both warehousing and transport emissions) prices the transport, facility, and
inventory components and lets the firm choose the speed-and-density mix that minimizes carbon across them.
\section{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{sec:discussion}

We have studied a distribution network that provides two services, availability and proximity, each of
which the firm can provide through more than one operational activity: safety stock or replenishment speed
for availability, facilities or last-mile distance for proximity. The carbon-minimizing configuration
depends on which activity provides each service at lower carbon intensity per dollar. The contribution of
the paper is to make these two substitutions explicit and to give the thresholds at which the
carbon-minimizing configuration departs from the cost-minimizing one.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The thresholds apply to an average, symmetric distribution environment, so their use
in a heterogeneous network is diagnostic rather than mechanical. Heterogeneous markets or discrete facility
sites replace $d(n)=\delta n^{-1/2}$ with the realized last-mile profile; this can move the density threshold,
but it leaves the underlying comparison between facility carbon per dollar and last-mile carbon per dollar
unchanged. The representative inbound distance isolates the proximity effect of adding DCs. If density also
changes inbound routing, shipment consolidation, or facility scale economies, those mechanisms add
centralizing forces and can move a near-boundary case toward consolidation. The base model keeps last-mile
rates fill-independent; the load-factor sensitivity of Section~\ref{sec:analysis} shows how this assumption
matters: common fill penalties consolidate both optima, while fill penalties that fall more heavily on carbon
can reverse the density comparison. Service enters as a cycle-service requirement; if service or fill rate is chosen
endogenously, safety stock becomes an additional design margin and the speed threshold should be evaluated at
the chosen service level. Finally, the calibration is regime-representative rather than firm-estimated, so the
numerical values illustrate the order of magnitude of the threshold movements under documented rate ranges.
The thresholds are therefore diagnostics to be evaluated with product- and network-specific rate data.

\paragraph{Future research.} The most direct extensions are those that can move the threshold boundaries.
Multi-product and multi-echelon settings would show how a shared network accommodates products in different
regimes and where safety stock should be held. Explicit load-factor, shipment-consolidation, and routing
models would quantify the centralizing force represented here by the load sensitivity. Heterogeneous geography
and discrete sites would replace the continuous density curve with network-specific last-mile profiles.
Empirical calibration with firm-level logistics-emissions data would estimate where real products fall
relative to the speed and density boundaries, and endogenous service choice would make the cycle-service target
a design decision rather than an input.

The model identifies which operational activity supplies availability and which supplies proximity at
lower carbon intensity per dollar, and shows how the two substitutions interact in the joint distribution
design: the speed threshold prices the availability margin, the pooling-adjusted density threshold prices
the proximity margin, and complementarity links the two in the final configuration.
